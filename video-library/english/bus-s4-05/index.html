Boston matrix and the product portfolio

Think of a café menu. Some items sell every single day. Some are new and still finding fans. Some hardly ever sell. All the products a business sells are called its product portfolio. The Boston matrix sorts these products using two measures. Market growth: is the market getting bigger? And market share: how much of that market does the product have? Growth runs up the side, and share runs along the bottom, with high share on the left.

Step one: place the product by asking two questions. Is market growth high or low? Is market share high or low? Step two: name the category and its decision. High growth and high share makes a star. Stars are usually worth investing in. Low growth but high share makes a cash cow. It brings in steady profit, which can fund other products. High growth but low share makes a question mark, sometimes called a problem child. Its future is uncertain. It may need investment to succeed, or it may be dropped. Low growth and low share makes a dog. Dogs are often dropped or replaced. Step three: link the decision to the whole portfolio. Profit from cash cows funds stars, question marks and new products, so the range stays balanced across several categories.

Here is a real question from Paper one in June 2022. Explain one benefit to a business of using the Boston matrix. It is worth three marks: one benefit, then two linked steps. Mark one is the benefit. The Boston matrix helps a business keep a balanced product portfolio. Mark two shows what the matrix reveals. Managers can see if they rely too heavily on a few cash cows, with too few stars or question marks coming through. Mark three gives the action and result. The business uses cash cow profit to launch new products before older ones fall away, so its income stays steady.

Mistake one: swapping the stars and cash cows. Both have high market share. The difference is growth: stars are in growing markets, cash cows are not. Mistake two: thinking the matrix shows profit directly. It measures market share and market growth.

Now you try. A games company has a football game with a large share of a market that has stopped growing. Where does it go on the Boston matrix, and what should the company do with it? Pause and decide.

Low growth and high share make it a cash cow. The company should keep selling it and use its steady profit to develop new games, perhaps for faster-growing markets. Remember the method: ask about growth, ask about share, then name the category and its decision. Now try the practice questions.
