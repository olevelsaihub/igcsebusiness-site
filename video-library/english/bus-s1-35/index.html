Measuring business success

Idea. How do you know if you did well in a test? You compare your score with your target, with your last test, and with your classmates. A business judges its success in the same way. Financial measures are about money: revenue from sales, profit, market share, and growth. Non-financial measures include customer satisfaction, shown by reviews and complaints; employee satisfaction, shown by staff turnover and absence; and owner satisfaction. One figure means little on its own. A profit of fifty thousand dollars looks good next to forty thousand last year, but weak next to a rival's ninety thousand.

Method. Step one: choose a measure that fits the business and what its owner wants. Step two: say what a rise or fall in that measure would show. Step three: compare the figure with last year, the target, or a rival. Step four: link the result to a decision, such as reinvesting profit or changing prices. Every measure has limits. Money measures are exact and easy to compare, but profit can rise while customers are unhappy. Satisfaction shows how people feel, but it is harder to measure.

Worked example. Here is a real question from Paper one in November 2021. Explain one measure a business can use to judge its success. It is worth three marks. Mark one is the measure: market share, the percentage of all sales in a market that a business makes. Mark two says what it shows. If market share rises from one year to the next, the business is winning customers from its competitors. Mark three says how it is used. Managers can plan with it, keeping a popular range, or changing prices or promotion if market share starts to fall.

Common mistake. Mistake one: giving an objective, such as to survive, when the question asks for a way to measure success. Mistake two: treating profit alone as proof of success, without looking at customers, staff or rivals.

Your turn. Now you try. A gym's revenue rose from eighty thousand dollars to ninety-six thousand dollars this year, but customer complaints doubled. Give one point showing success and one point against. Pause and decide.

Answer. For success: revenue rose by sixteen thousand dollars, so more money is coming in from members. Against: complaints doubled, so customers are less satisfied and may leave next year, which could make revenue fall. Revenue alone does not prove success. Remember the method: choose a measure, say what it shows, compare it, then link it to a decision. Now try the practice questions for this type.
