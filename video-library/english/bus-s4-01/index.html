Market research methods: primary and secondary

Idea. Imagine you want to sell fresh juice at your school fair. Before you spend your savings, you would want to know who will buy, which flavours they like, and what price they will pay. Finding this out is called market research. It means collecting information about customers, competitors and the market, so a business can make better decisions. There are two kinds. Primary research, also called field research, is new information the business collects itself. Secondary research, also called desk research, uses information that already exists, collected by someone else.

Method. Step one: sort the method. Surveys, questionnaires, focus groups, observation and test marketing are primary. The internet, market reports and government reports are secondary. Two methods need a closer look. A focus group is a small group of people who discuss a product in depth. Test marketing means selling a product in one small area before a full launch. Step two: recall a strength and a weakness. Primary research is up to date and specific to the business, but it costs more and takes longer. Secondary research is cheaper and quicker, but it may be out of date, not exactly what is needed, and rivals can see it too. Step three: link it to the business in the question: who would be asked, where, and about which product. Then build a chain: the point, why, and the result for the business.

Worked example. Here is a real question from Paper one in June 2021. Explain one benefit to a business of using secondary market research. It is worth three marks: one benefit, then two linked steps. Mark one is the benefit. Secondary research is cheap. Mark two explains why. The information already exists, for example in a government report or on the internet, so the business does not pay for its own survey. Mark three gives the result. The money saved can be spent on other parts of the business, such as developing or promoting its products.

Common mistake. Mistake one: calling internet research or government reports primary. That information already exists, so it is secondary. Mistake two: saying primary research is cheaper or quicker. It is the other way round. Primary is up to date and specific, but dearer and slower.

Your turn. Now you try. A new bakery asks fifty shoppers to taste its vegan cakes and answer a few questions. Is this primary or secondary research? Give one drawback for the bakery. Pause and decide.

Answer. It is primary research, because the bakery is collecting new information itself. One drawback: staff must spend time running the tasting and giving away cakes, so it costs more and is slower than reading a report. That is the method for every question on this type: sort the method, give a strength or weakness, then link it to the business. Now try the practice questions.
