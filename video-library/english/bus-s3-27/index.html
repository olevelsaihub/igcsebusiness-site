Liquidity and how useful accounting ratios are

Read the narration

Imagine you own an old phone worth two hundred dollars, but you need five dollars for lunch today. You cannot pay the canteen with a phone. You would have to sell it first, and that takes time. Liquidity is how easily a business can turn its assets into cash to pay its short-term debts. It is not just how much cash it has. There are two families of ratios. Liquidity ratios, the current ratio and the acid test ratio, show whether short-term debts can be paid. Profitability ratios, such as gross and operating profit margin, show how much profit is made from each unit of sales.

To judge how useful a ratio is, follow four steps. Say what it measures. Quote the figures and the trend. Link the trend to a decision, such as paying suppliers or arranging finance. Then say what the ratio cannot show. Ratios are most useful when compared with previous years, with similar businesses, or with a benchmark such as one. But they use past figures, and they do not explain why a change happened.

Here is a real question from Paper one, June twenty twenty-two, about The Better Toy Store, or TBTS, a toy shop in Singapore. Analyse the usefulness to TBTS of the acid test ratio. It is worth six marks. The acid test ratio shows whether TBTS could pay its short-term debts without selling its toys. The figure shows it rising from zero point seven five in twenty eighteen, to zero point eight seven, then zero point eight eight in twenty twenty. It is rising, but always below one. Point one. Liquidity is improving, so TBTS is more able to pay its toy suppliers on time. That keeps a good relationship, so suppliers are more willing to keep giving credit. Point two. Below one means TBTS has less than one dollar of quick assets for every dollar it owes soon. So it must be careful with cash before buying new stock, or arrange an overdraft in advance. Also, the rise slowed from zero point one two to zero point zero one, and the ratio does not explain why.

Mistake one: defining liquidity as how much cash a business has. Liquidity is about turning assets into cash to pay short-term debts. Mistake two: saying a rising ratio means all is well, when it is still below one. Rising is good news, but below one still means caution.

Now you try. A sports shop's current ratio was two point one in twenty twenty-three, one point six in twenty twenty-four, and one point two in twenty twenty-five. What does the trend show, and what is one action the shop could take? Pause and decide.

The current ratio is falling every year, so liquidity is getting worse, although it is still above one. The shop should act now, for example by selling equipment it no longer needs to raise cash, or by swapping its overdraft for a long-term loan, so it can keep paying its suppliers on time. Remember the method: say what the ratio measures, quote the figures and trend, link it to a decision, and say what it cannot show. Now try the practice questions for this type.
