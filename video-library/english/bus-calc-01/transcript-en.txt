When you travel abroad, you swap your money for the local currency. The exchange rate tells you how much of one currency you get for another. For example, one euro equals five Malaysian ringgits. So one euro buys five ringgits. So ten euros buys ten times five, which is fifty ringgits. Going from the currency with the one, we multiply. Going the other way, we divide. Fifty ringgits is fifty divided by five, which is ten euros.

Step one: write the rate with a one in front, and underline the currency the answer must be in. Rates can be written either way round, so check every time. Step two: work out the total in the starting currency first. For example, six bicycles at two hundred euros each cost one thousand two hundred euros. Step three: from the one currency, multiply by the rate. To go back to the one currency, divide by the rate. Step four: round as asked, usually to two decimal places, and write the currency. Step five: sense check. If the new currency is worth less, you get more of it, so your number should get bigger.

Here is a real question from Paper one R in June 2019. Lavender, a Malaysian business, buys one hundred and fifty kilograms of yeast from Germany at a cost of two thousand two hundred and fifty euros. The rate is one euro equals five ringgits. How much does it pay in ringgits? It is worth one mark. The euro has the one, and we want ringgits, so we multiply. Two thousand two hundred and fifty times five is eleven thousand two hundred and fifty ringgits. Sense check: each euro is worth five ringgits, so the answer should be bigger, and it is. Choose option C. The one hundred and fifty kilograms is extra information. This explanation is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

The most common mistake is dividing when you should multiply. Two thousand two hundred and fifty divided by five gives four hundred and fifty, which fails the sense check. Another mistake is converting the wrong number. One hundred and fifty times five is seven hundred and fifty, which is trap option A, but kilograms are not money. Convert the cost.

Now you try. A shop in the UK buys forty lamps from China at two hundred and fifty Chinese yuan each. The exchange rate is one pound equals nine yuan. Calculate the total cost in pounds, to two decimal places. Pause here and work it out.

First the total in yuan. Forty times two hundred and fifty is ten thousand yuan. The pound has the one, and we want pounds, so we divide. Ten thousand divided by nine is one thousand one hundred and eleven pounds and eleven pence. Sense check: a pound is worth more than a yuan, so the number got smaller. Remember the method: find the currency with the one, work out the total first, then multiply or divide and check the size. Now try the practice questions for this type.
