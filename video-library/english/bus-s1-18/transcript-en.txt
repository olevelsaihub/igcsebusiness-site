Idea. When you go on holiday, you swap your money for the local currency. Suppose one pound buys one euro twenty. That is an exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another. If one pound now buys one euro thirty, the pound has got stronger. This is appreciation. If it buys only one euro ten, the pound has got weaker. This is depreciation. Businesses are affected when they trade abroad. An importer buys goods or materials from another country. An exporter sells goods or services to another country.

Method. Here is the rule. A stronger home currency makes imports cheaper and exports dearer for foreign buyers. A weaker home currency makes imports dearer and exports cheaper. To remember it: strong currency, cheap imports, dear exports. A weak currency is the opposite. Let's prove it. A British firm sells a jumper for fifty pounds. At one euro twenty, a European buyer pays sixty euros. If the pound weakens to one euro ten, they pay only fifty-five euros. The export is now cheaper for them. So the method is: decide the direction, work out what happens to import costs, work out what happens to export prices, then follow each effect to sales, costs or profit for this business.

Worked example. Here is a real question from Paper one, November twenty twenty-three, about As We Grow, or AWG, a clothing business in Iceland. It imports materials and exports its clothes. Analyse the impact on AWG of a depreciation of the Icelandic krona. It is worth six marks. Point one: imports. A depreciation means the krona is weaker, so AWG must pay more krona for the same imported material. Its cost of making each jumper rises, so it may raise prices or accept a smaller profit. Point two: exports. Overseas customers need less of their own currency to buy an AWG jumper, so its clothes are cheaper for them. Demand from abroad may rise, increasing AWG's export revenue. Overall, the impact depends on which effect is larger.

Common mistake. Mistake one: getting the direction wrong. A weaker currency makes imports dearer, not cheaper. Mistake two: only writing about imports when the business also exports. If the case says it does both, use both.

Your turn. Now you try. A furniture maker in Malaysia exports tables to Australia. The Malaysian ringgit appreciates, so it gets stronger. What happens to the price Australian customers pay, and to the firm's sales? Pause and decide.

Answer. A stronger ringgit makes exports dearer. Australian customers must pay more Australian dollars for the same table, so some may buy elsewhere. The firm's export sales and revenue may fall. Remember the method: decide the direction, then follow imports and exports through to sales, costs and profit for the business. Now try the practice questions for this type.
