Training: induction, on-the-job and off-the-job

Think about your first day at a new school. Someone shows you around, tells you the rules and what to do in a fire drill. After that, you learn by doing, with help from classmates. Training means teaching employees the skills and knowledge they need to do their job well. There are three types: induction, on-the-job and off-the-job.

Induction training is for new employees. It introduces the workplace, colleagues, the rules, and health and safety procedures. On-the-job training happens at the workplace while the person works, for example shadowing an experienced colleague. It is usually cheaper and fits the business. Off-the-job training happens away from the workstation, such as at a college. It can bring new techniques, but costs more and takes staff away. Training brings higher skills and productivity, better quality and service, and motivated staff who stay. But it costs money and time, output can fall while staff train, and trained staff may leave for a competitor. To explain, identify the type of training, give one benefit or drawback that fits it, then link it to productivity, quality or customer service. Use the job in the case.

Here is a real question from Paper two in June twenty twenty-five. Explain one disadvantage for a business of training its employees. Three marks. Notice that it asks for a disadvantage. Mark one is the disadvantage. Training costs the business money. Mark two explains where the cost comes from. The business pays for a course or a trainer's time, and still pays the trainees' wages while they learn instead of work. Mark three is the consequence. Costs rise, and because less work gets done during training, output falls, so profit may fall in the short term. This explanation is by olevels.com, not an official mark scheme.

A common mistake is mixing up the two types. On-the-job happens at work while working. Off-the-job happens away from the workstation. Another mistake is giving a benefit when the question asks for a disadvantage, or just defining the type. Neither answers the question.

Now you try. A new waiter starts at a busy restaurant. The manager wants him to learn to serve tables quickly and cheaply. Which type of training should he get, and why? Pause and decide.

On-the-job training, by shadowing an experienced waiter. He learns this restaurant's own menu and routines while he works, so it costs little, and he can serve customers sooner. He should also have induction on health and safety first. Remember the method: identify the type of training, give one benefit or drawback that fits it, then link it to the business.
