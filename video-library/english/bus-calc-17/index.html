Calculate the current ratio

Idea. Imagine you have thirty dollars in your pocket, and you owe a friend twenty dollars by Friday. Can you pay? Yes, with some to spare. Divide what you have by what you owe. Thirty divided by twenty is one point five. You have one dollar fifty for every one dollar you owe. We write this as one point five to one. A business does the same check. Current assets are things it owns that will turn into cash within a year: cash, inventory, and money owed by customers. Current liabilities are debts it must pay within a year, such as money owed to suppliers or an overdraft.

Method. The current ratio is current assets divided by current liabilities. The formula is given in the exam, but you must put the right figures in the right places. Assets go on top. Step one, find both figures for the year the question asks about. Step two, divide. You do not take anything away. Step three, round as the question says, usually to two decimal places, and write it against one. What does the answer mean? Above one, current assets are bigger than current liabilities, so they could cover the short-term debts. Below one, they could not.

Worked example. Here is a real question from Paper one, June twenty twenty-three, about a chocolate shop. Its current assets are twenty-six thousand euros and its current liabilities are fourteen thousand eight hundred and fifty-two euros. Calculate the current ratio to two decimal places. It is worth two marks. Set up the formula with the figures. Twenty-six thousand on top, fourteen thousand eight hundred and fifty-two underneath. Divide. The calculator shows one point seven five zero six and more. To two decimal places, that is one point seven five, or one point seven five to one. So the shop has one euro seventy-five of current assets for every one euro it owes in the short term. That is above one.

Common mistake. The most common mistake is turning the ratio upside down: liabilities divided by assets. That gives zero point five seven, which is wrong. Another mistake is taking inventory away first. That gives the acid test ratio, a different calculation. For the current ratio, use all the current assets.

Your turn. Now you try. A café has current assets of nine thousand three hundred pounds and current liabilities of four thousand two hundred pounds. Calculate its current ratio to two decimal places. Pause here and work it out.

Answer. Nine thousand three hundred divided by four thousand two hundred is two point two one four and more. To two decimal places, two point two one, or two point two one to one. The café has more than twice what it owes in the short term. Remember the method: find the two figures, divide assets by liabilities, then round and write it against one.
