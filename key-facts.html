Key facts by topic

1 Business activity and influences on business

Meaning of stakeholder and shareholder
  • A stakeholder is any person or group that is affected by, or has an interest in, what a business does.
  • Examples of stakeholders: owners, employees, customers, suppliers, the local community, lenders and the government.
  • A shareholder is a person or organisation that owns one or more shares in a company, so is a part-owner of it.
  • All shareholders are stakeholders, but not all stakeholders are shareholders.

Open the lesson and practice

Primary, secondary and tertiary sectors
  • Primary sector: taking raw materials from the earth or sea, for example farming, fishing, mining and forestry.
  • Secondary sector: turning raw materials into finished or part-finished goods, for example manufacturing, building and engineering.
  • Tertiary sector: providing services, for example shops (retail), banks, insurance, hotels, restaurants and transport.
  • A shop that sells goods is tertiary, because selling is a service even though the products are goods.
  • To classify a business, ask what it actually does: extract, make, or serve.

Open the lesson and practice

Social enterprises: meaning and purpose
  • A social enterprise is a business that sells goods or services but has a main aim of helping people or the environment.
  • Most of its profit is put back into its social or environmental purpose, or into the community, rather than paid out to owners.
  • People set one up because they want to help their community or a cause while still running a business.
  • A social objective is an aim to benefit society; a social enterprise is a whole business built around such aims.

Open the lesson and practice

Business objectives: financial, non-financial and social
  • An objective is a target a business sets so it knows what it is trying to achieve.
  • Financial objectives are about money: survival, profit, sales (revenue), market share and financial security.
  • Non-financial objectives are not mainly about money: social objectives, personal satisfaction, challenge, independence and control.
  • A social objective is an aim to help society or the environment, for example cutting waste or supporting a local community.
  • Setting objectives gives the owner and staff a clear direction, motivates them, and gives a target to check progress against.
  • Objectives change as a business evolves: because of market conditions, new technology, its own results, new laws, or internal reasons such as growth or a new owner.

Open the lesson and practice

Forms of business ownership and limited liability
  • Sole trader: a business owned by one person, who keeps all the profit, has full control and has unlimited liability.
  • Partnership: owned by two or more partners who share decisions, skills, capital and profit; usually unlimited liability.
  • Private limited company (Ltd): owned by shareholders, shares are not sold to the public, owners have limited liability.
  • Public limited company (plc): shares can be bought and sold by the public on the stock exchange, so it can raise large amounts of capital.
  • Public corporation: a business owned and run by the government (the state), usually to provide a service rather than to maximise profit.
  • Unlimited liability: the owner can lose personal possessions to pay business debts. Limited liability: owners can only lose the money they invested.
  • A sole trader pays income tax on its profits; companies pay corporation tax.

Open the lesson and practice

Franchising: franchisor and franchisee
  • A franchise is when one business lets another person or business trade under its name, sell its products and use its way of working.
  • The franchisor owns the brand and sells the right to use it; the franchisee buys that right and runs the outlet.
  • The franchisee pays a set-up fee at the start and usually ongoing royalties, often a percentage of sales or profit.
  • Benefits to a franchisee: a brand customers already know, training and support, a business idea that is already proven, so less risk of failure.
  • Drawbacks to a franchisee: fees reduce profit, there is no guaranteed wage, and it must follow the franchisor's rules and buy from approved suppliers.
  • Benefits to a franchisor: fast growth using the franchisees' money, fee and royalty income, and its brand spreading to new areas.
  • Drawbacks to a franchisor: less control over quality, and one badly run outlet can damage the whole brand.

Open the lesson and practice

Government spending, taxation and laws
  • The government spends money to provide public services, such as the police, schools, hospitals and roads.
  • Taxes are the main way a government raises money (revenue) to pay for this spending.
  • Legislation means laws passed by the government, often to protect people such as customers, workers or the public.
  • Laws can force businesses to change what they do, for example health and safety rules or limits on ingredients.
  • Following new laws can raise a business's costs (training, new equipment, changing products), but can also protect its reputation.

Open the lesson and practice

Reasons why businesses fail
  • Business failure means a business has to stop trading and close.
  • Cash-flow problems: more cash goes out than comes in, so bills, wages and suppliers cannot be paid on time.
  • Lack of finance: the business cannot raise the money it needs to keep going or to grow.
  • Not being competitive: rivals offer better prices, quality or service, so customers go elsewhere.
  • Failing to adapt: the business does not change when customer tastes, technology or the market change.
  • Insolvency means the business cannot pay its debts when they are due.

Open the lesson and practice

Trading blocs and tariffs
  • A trading bloc is a group of countries that agree to remove trade barriers, such as tariffs, between themselves.
  • A tariff is a tax on imported goods, which makes them more expensive in the importing country.
  • Inside a bloc, goods move between members without tariffs, so trade is cheaper and easier.
  • Blocs often keep tariffs on goods from non-member countries to protect their own businesses.
  • Benefits to a country: cheaper imports, more choice for consumers, bigger markets for its exporters, more jobs.

Open the lesson and practice

Effects of interest rate changes
  • An interest rate is the percentage charged for borrowing money, or paid as a reward for saving it.
  • When interest rates rise, loans and overdrafts cost more to repay, so business costs rise.
  • Higher rates also mean customers with loans have less money to spend, and saving becomes more attractive, so demand can fall.
  • When interest rates fall, borrowing is cheaper, so businesses may invest and expand, and customers may spend more.
  • Businesses selling expensive or non-essential items are usually hit hardest by higher rates.

Open the lesson and practice

External factors affecting business decisions
  • External factors are things outside a business's control that affect its decisions.
  • Social factors: changes in people's lifestyles and tastes, such as becoming more health conscious.
  • Technological factors: new technology, such as online selling or new machines.
  • Environmental factors: concern about pollution, waste, recycling and climate, and pressure to be 'green'.
  • Political factors: government actions, such as taxation and laws.
  • Businesses respond by changing products, methods or marketing, which can raise costs but improve reputation and sales.

Open the lesson and practice

Choosing where to locate a business
  • Location means where a business places its factory, shop, office or other premises.
  • Closeness to the market: being near customers increases sales and lowers delivery costs.
  • Closeness to labour: being near enough suitable workers, with the right skills, at an affordable wage.
  • Closeness to materials: being near suppliers lowers transport costs and avoids delays.
  • Competitors: some firms locate away from rivals; others cluster near them where customers already shop.
  • Other factors: the type of business, cost of land or rent, the internet (selling online means location matters less), laws and trading blocs.

Open the lesson and practice

Multinationals and globalisation
  • A multinational is a business that operates (produces or sells) in more than one country.
  • Globalisation is the growth of trade and business links across the whole world, so firms buy, make and sell in many countries.
  • Globalisation gives even small businesses opportunities, such as selling to customers abroad online, but also threats, such as more foreign competition.
  • Benefits to a multinational: more customers and sales, lower costs by producing where it is cheapest, economies of scale, risk spread across countries, and access to skilled staff.
  • A large group can own businesses in several markets; earnings and brand strength from one part can support the others.
  • Benefits to a host country (where the multinational locates): jobs, new skills and training, tax revenue for public services, and investment in infrastructure.
  • Possible drawbacks to a host country: profits sent abroad, local firms squeezed out, low wages or harm to the environment.

Open the lesson and practice

Effects of exchange rate changes
  • An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another, for example how many euros one pound buys.
  • An importer buys goods or materials from another country; an exporter sells goods or services to another country.
  • Appreciation: the home currency gets stronger, so it buys more foreign currency.
  • Depreciation: the home currency gets weaker, so it buys less foreign currency.
  • A stronger currency makes imports cheaper and exports dearer for foreign buyers.
  • A weaker currency makes imports dearer and exports cheaper for foreign buyers, so exporters may sell more.

Open the lesson and practice

Measuring business success
  • Success means how well a business is doing: compared with its own objectives, with its past results, and with its rivals.
  • Financial measures: revenue (money from sales), profit (revenue minus costs), market share (the business's sales as a percentage of all sales in its market) and growth (more sales, outlets, products or staff).
  • Non-financial measures: customer satisfaction (reviews, complaints, repeat visits), employee satisfaction (staff turnover, absence, surveys) and owner or shareholder satisfaction (dividends, goals met).
  • A single figure means little on its own; it becomes useful when compared over time, against the target set, or with competitors.
  • Every measure has limits: profit can rise while customers are unhappy, and satisfaction is harder to measure accurately than money.
  • Missing expected growth has costs: shareholders may be unhappy and invest elsewhere, and the business may have less money to expand and may lose customers to rivals.

Open the lesson and practice

2 People in business

Dismissing an employee
  • Dismissal means the employer ends a worker's employment because of something the worker has done or cannot do.
  • Fair reasons include serious misconduct (theft, violence, breaking safety rules), repeated lateness or absence, and being unable to do the job properly.
  • Businesses usually follow a disciplinary procedure first, such as warnings.
  • Unfair dismissal is ending someone's job for a reason the law does not allow, such as discrimination.
  • Redundancy is different: the job itself is no longer needed, so it is not the worker's fault.

Open the lesson and practice

Equal opportunities and minimum wage laws
  • Discrimination means treating someone unfairly because of who they are.
  • Equal opportunities laws protect people from discrimination because of gender, race, disability, religion, sexual orientation and age.
  • Skills, ability, experience, political views and 'ethical' or 'social' reasons are not the protected characteristics listed in the syllabus.
  • Businesses must treat people fairly in recruitment, pay, training and promotion; for example equal pay for the same work and open access to training.
  • Businesses may need to make reasonable changes so disabled people can work, such as ramps, adapted equipment or flexible hours.
  • The minimum wage is the lowest pay per hour that a business is legally allowed to pay a worker.
  • Breaking these laws can lead to court or tribunal cases, fines or compensation, and damage to the business's reputation.

Open the lesson and practice

Functional areas and the human resources role
  • A functional area (department) is a part of a business that carries out one type of work.
  • Human resources (HR) deals with people: workforce planning, recruitment and selection, training, health and safety, staff welfare, discipline, grievances and dismissal.
  • Finance deals with money: paying wages and salaries, paying suppliers, sending invoices, budgets, cash-flow forecasts and accounts.
  • Marketing deals with customers: market research, pricing, advertising, sales promotion, public relations, customer service and distribution.
  • Production makes the product: manufacturing, designing new products, quality control and stock control.
  • Having an HR function means the business has the right number of suitable, trained staff and follows employment law.

Open the lesson and practice

Recruitment documents: job description to CV
  • Recruitment means finding and attracting people to apply for a job; selection means choosing between them.
  • A job description is written by the business and lists the job title, tasks, duties and responsibilities of the job.
  • A person specification is written by the business and lists the qualifications, skills, experience and qualities the ideal person should have.
  • An application form is designed by the business and filled in by the applicant, so every applicant gives the same information in the same order.
  • A curriculum vitae (CV) is written by the applicant and summarises their education, qualifications, work experience and referees.
  • Together these documents help the business compare applicants fairly and choose someone who matches the job.

Open the lesson and practice

Health and safety at work
  • Health and safety laws require businesses to protect employees and customers from harm at work.
  • A hazard is anything that could cause harm, such as hot equipment, trailing wires or heavy machinery.
  • Ways to comply: safety training (often in induction), protective clothing, safety signs, checking equipment and clear emergency procedures.
  • Complying reduces accidents and injuries, so fewer staff are off sick.
  • A business that breaks the law can be fined, sued or even closed down, and its reputation can suffer.
  • Staff who feel safe are more confident and motivated, and customers are more willing to use the business.

Open the lesson and practice

Motivation theories: Maslow, Taylor and Herzberg
  • Motivation is the desire to work hard and do a job well.
  • Taylor believed workers are motivated mainly by money, so pay should be linked to output (for example piece rate).
  • Maslow's hierarchy of needs has five levels, from bottom to top: basic (physiological), safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation.
  • In Maslow's theory a worker must meet lower needs before higher needs motivate them; social needs are level 3.
  • Herzberg split factors into hygiene factors (pay, working conditions, job security) and motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility, promotion).
  • Herzberg said missing hygiene factors cause dissatisfaction, but only motivators truly motivate workers.

Open the lesson and practice

Why motivating and keeping staff matters
  • Motivated employees work harder, make fewer mistakes and give better customer service.
  • Retaining staff means keeping employees so they do not leave the business.
  • Staff turnover is the rate at which employees leave and have to be replaced; high turnover is costly.
  • Replacing staff costs time and money for advertising, interviewing and training.
  • Experienced staff who stay build up product knowledge and skills they can pass on to customers and colleagues.
  • Paying above the minimum wage can attract better applicants, reward highly skilled staff and stop staff moving to competitors.
  • Higher productivity and better service can lead to more sales and higher profit.

Open the lesson and practice

Communication: methods, barriers and why it matters
  • Communication is passing a message from a sender to a receiver, who must understand it and may reply (feedback).
  • Internal communication happens inside the business, for example a manager talking to staff at a team meeting.
  • External communication is with people outside the business, such as customers, suppliers or the bank.
  • Methods include face-to-face meetings, letters, email, noticeboards, phone calls and video calls; each differs in cost, speed, privacy and whether it leaves a written record.
  • A barrier is anything that stops a message being received or understood: jargon, language differences, noise, a long chain of command, poor technology or a sender who is not trusted.
  • Ineffective communication leads to mistakes, wasted time, demotivated staff and unhappy customers.
  • Barriers can be removed by using simple language, choosing the right method, asking for feedback and shortening the chain of command.

Open the lesson and practice

Internal and external recruitment
  • Internal recruitment fills a vacancy with someone who already works for the business, often by promotion.
  • External recruitment fills a vacancy with someone from outside the business, found through advertising, agencies or websites.
  • Internal benefits: the person already knows the business, it is quicker and cheaper, less training is needed, and promotion can motivate staff.
  • Internal drawbacks: a smaller choice of candidates, no new ideas, and another vacancy is left behind.
  • External benefits: a wider pool of applicants, new skills and fresh ideas.
  • External drawbacks: advertising, shortlisting and interviewing cost time and money, the new person needs induction, and existing staff may feel overlooked.
  • A job advertisement should include the job title, pay, main duties, required skills and how to apply.

Open the lesson and practice

Selection: shortlisting and interviewing candidates
  • Selection is choosing the best person from the people who applied for a job.
  • Shortlisting means picking out the applicants who best match the job description and person specification, so only they are invited for interview.
  • An interview is a meeting where the business asks the candidate questions to judge their skills, knowledge, attitude and how well they would fit in.
  • Interviews let the business check that what the applicant wrote on the CV or application form is true.
  • Good selection avoids a poor hire, which would waste money on wages, training and recruiting again.

Open the lesson and practice

Span of control, chain of command and delegation
  • Span of control is the number of workers a manager is directly responsible for.
  • A narrow (short) span means few workers per manager: close supervision but more managers and more layers.
  • A wide span means many workers per manager: less supervision, more trust and fewer managers.
  • Chain of command is the line of authority that instructions pass down, from the top of the business to the workers.
  • A short chain of command means fewer levels, so messages pass faster and are less likely to be distorted.
  • Delegation means a manager passes authority to carry out a task to a subordinate (someone lower in the structure).
  • Delegation frees the manager's time and can motivate staff who feel trusted.
  • A wide span can empower staff and speed up decisions, but a manager with too many staff may not supervise everyone well, so productivity can fall.

Open the lesson and practice

Training: induction, on-the-job and off-the-job
  • Training means teaching employees the skills and knowledge they need to do their job well.
  • Induction training is for new employees: it introduces the workplace, colleagues, rules, and health and safety procedures.
  • On-the-job training happens at the workplace while the person works, for example shadowing an experienced colleague.
  • Off-the-job training happens away from the normal workstation, for example at a college or with outside experts.
  • Benefits of training: higher skills and productivity, better quality and customer service, more motivated staff who are more likely to stay.
  • Limitations: training costs money and time, output can fall while staff train, and trained staff may leave for a competitor.
  • On-the-job training is usually cheaper and specific to the business; off-the-job training can bring new techniques but costs more and takes staff away.

Open the lesson and practice

Types of employment: full-time, part-time, temporary
  • Full-time employees work the business's normal working week, often around 35-40 hours.
  • Part-time employees work fewer hours than full-time staff, for example only mornings or weekends.
  • Job share means one full-time job is split between two part-time workers who share the hours and duties.
  • Casual staff are called in only when needed; seasonal staff work at busy times of year; temporary staff are hired for a fixed period, for example to cover illness.
  • Permanent, full-time staff give stability, commitment and experience, but must be paid even in quiet periods.
  • Part-time, casual, seasonal and temporary staff give flexibility and lower wage costs in quiet periods, but may be less committed and need more training.

Open the lesson and practice

Financial and non-financial methods of motivation
  • Financial methods involve money: remuneration (pay for work), bonus (extra payment for reaching a target), commission (pay based on how much an employee sells), promotion (a higher job with higher pay), and fringe benefits.
  • Fringe benefits are extras on top of normal pay, such as staff discounts, a company car, free gym use or health insurance.
  • Non-financial methods do not involve extra money: job rotation, job enrichment and autonomy.
  • Job rotation means employees move between different jobs or departments for periods of time, reducing boredom.
  • Job enrichment means giving employees more challenging tasks and responsibility so the job is more interesting.
  • Autonomy means giving workers the freedom to decide how they do their own work.
  • Financial methods can quickly raise effort but add costs; non-financial methods can build skills and loyalty but may not suit everyone.

Open the lesson and practice

Organisational structures: hierarchical, flat, centralised, decentralised
  • An organisational chart shows the structure of a business: who is responsible to whom.
  • A hierarchical (tall) structure has many levels, a long chain of command and narrow spans of control.
  • Hierarchical benefits: close supervision and more chances of promotion; drawbacks: slow communication and decisions, and higher management costs.
  • A flat structure has few levels, a short chain of command and wide spans of control: faster communication and lower costs, but managers may be overloaded.
  • Centralised means important decisions are made by senior managers at head office.
  • Decentralised means decisions are passed down to managers in branches or regions, who can react to local customers.
  • Centralised gives control and consistency; decentralised gives speed, local knowledge and more motivated staff.

Open the lesson and practice

3 Business finance

Fixed and variable costs
  • A cost is money a business spends to produce and sell its goods or services.
  • Output is the number of goods or services a business makes or provides.
  • Fixed costs stay the same whatever the output, e.g. rent, insurance and managers' salaries.
  • Variable costs change directly with output, e.g. raw materials and packaging: make more, spend more.
  • Total cost is fixed costs plus variable costs.
  • To classify a cost, ask: would this bill rise if the business made one more unit?

Open the lesson and practice

Revenue and profit: meaning and how to raise them
  • Revenue is the money a business receives from selling its goods or services over a period: price multiplied by the number sold.
  • Profit is what is left when total costs are taken away from total revenue; if costs are bigger, the business makes a loss.
  • Gross profit is revenue minus cost of sales (the cost of the goods sold); the gross profit margin is gross profit as a percentage of revenue.
  • Profit can rise by increasing revenue (sell more, or raise the price if customers keep buying) or by cutting costs.
  • Each method has a trade-off: a higher price may lose customers; cutting costs may lower quality.
  • Profit matters because it rewards owners and shareholders, can be reinvested to grow, and shows lenders the business is doing well.

Open the lesson and practice

Statement of financial position: assets and liabilities
  • The statement of financial position shows what a business owns and what it owes on a particular date.
  • Assets are things the business owns. Non-current assets are kept for more than a year, e.g. buildings, machinery and vehicles.
  • Current assets are cash or items that will become cash within a year, e.g. cash, inventory and money owed by customers.
  • Inventory is the stock of goods or materials a business holds to sell or to use in production.
  • Liabilities are debts the business owes. Current liabilities must be paid within twelve months, e.g. an overdraft or money owed to suppliers; non-current liabilities are due after twelve months, e.g. a long-term loan.
  • Capital employed is the long-term money invested in the business.
  • Sales, cost of sales and gross profit belong in the income statement, not the statement of financial position.

Open the lesson and practice

Why cash matters and how to improve cash flow
  • Cash is the money a business actually has available to spend now, in the bank or in hand.
  • Cash flow is the movement of money into the business (inflows, e.g. from customers) and out of it (outflows, e.g. to suppliers).
  • A business needs cash to pay suppliers, overheads (regular running costs such as rent and electricity) and employees' wages.
  • Insolvency is when a business cannot pay its debts when they are due; it can lead to the business closing.
  • Cash and profit are different: a profitable business can still run out of cash if customers pay late.
  • Cash flow can be improved quickly by paying suppliers later, getting customers to pay sooner, or cutting stock levels.
  • Paying suppliers on time builds trust, which helps the business get credit and good service in future.

Open the lesson and practice

Cash-flow forecasts: terms, uses and limits
  • A cash-flow forecast is a plan that estimates the money expected to come into and go out of a business each month in the future.
  • Cash inflows are money coming in (e.g. sales, loans); cash outflows are money going out (e.g. wages, rent, supplies).
  • Net cash flow is cash inflows minus cash outflows for a period; it is negative when outflows are bigger than inflows.
  • The opening balance is the cash at the start of a month; the closing balance is the cash at the end, and it becomes next month's opening balance.
  • Forecasts help a business spot months when cash may run short, so it can arrange finance such as an overdraft in time.
  • Banks often ask to see a forecast before lending money.
  • A forecast is only an estimate: if sales or costs turn out differently, the forecast will be wrong.

Open the lesson and practice

Sources of finance and why businesses need finance
  • Finance is the money a business needs to start up, to run day to day, or to grow (expand).
  • Short-term finance is needed for less than a year, e.g. to pay bills when cash is low; long-term finance is needed for big items such as a new factory.
  • Internal sources come from inside the business or its owner: personal savings, retained profit (profit kept from earlier years) and selling assets the business no longer needs.
  • External sources come from outside: overdraft (permission to take more out of a bank account than is in it), trade credit (buying now and paying the supplier later), bank loans, share capital, venture capital and crowdfunding.
  • Share capital is money raised by selling shares (parts of ownership) in a company; only companies can do this, and a public limited company can sell shares on the stock market.
  • Venture capital is money from specialist investors who put money into new or risky businesses in return for a share of ownership, and often give advice too.
  • Crowdfunding is raising money from a large number of people, usually through a website, each giving a small amount.
  • Each source differs in cost (interest or a share of profit), how fast it arrives, whether it must be repaid, and whether the owners lose some control.

Open the lesson and practice

Break-even: meaning, charts and usefulness
  • Break-even is the level of output where total revenue equals total costs, so the business makes neither a profit nor a loss.
  • The break-even output is found with: fixed costs divided by (selling price minus variable cost per unit).
  • On a break-even chart, the break-even point is where the total revenue line crosses the total cost line; the fixed cost line is flat.
  • Above break-even output the business makes a profit; below it, a loss.
  • Higher fixed costs or variable costs raise the break-even point; a higher selling price lowers it.
  • Uses: shows how many units must be sold, lets managers ask 'what if' questions, and supports loan applications.
  • Limits: it assumes everything made is sold, costs and revenue rise in straight lines, and figures are only predictions.

Open the lesson and practice

Using income statements and financial documents to decide
  • The statement of comprehensive income (income statement) shows revenue, costs and profit over a period, usually a year.
  • Its main features are sales (revenue), cost of sales, gross profit, expenses and operating profit.
  • Gross profit is sales minus cost of sales; operating profit is gross profit minus expenses.
  • Financial documents help a business judge its performance by comparing figures with past years or with similar businesses.
  • Managers use them to decide whether to expand, launch products, cut costs or close loss-making parts.
  • Banks look at them to judge whether a loan can be repaid; shareholders look at them to judge profits and likely dividends.
  • They show past results only, so they cannot guarantee what will happen next.

Open the lesson and practice

Liquidity and how useful accounting ratios are
  • Liquidity is how easily a business can turn its assets into cash to pay its short-term debts.
  • The current ratio is current assets divided by current liabilities.
  • The acid test ratio is current assets minus inventory, divided by current liabilities; it leaves out stock because stock may be slow to sell.
  • An acid test ratio below 1 means the business has less than one unit of quick assets for every unit it owes in the short term.
  • Profitability ratios, such as gross profit margin and operating profit margin, show how much profit is made from each unit of sales.
  • Ratios are most useful when compared with previous years or with similar businesses.
  • Ratios use past figures and do not explain why a change happened.

Open the lesson and practice

4 Marketing

Mass marketing and niche marketing
  • Mass marketing means selling the same product to the whole market, aiming at as many customers as possible.
  • Niche marketing means targeting a small, specialised segment of the market with a product made for it.
  • Mass marketing can bring very high sales and lower average costs through large-scale production (economies of scale).
  • Mass marketing usually needs large promotion budgets and faces many competitors.
  • Niche marketing can allow higher prices and strong loyalty, with fewer competitors.
  • Niche markets are small, so total sales are limited and the business depends on one group.

Open the lesson and practice

Product life cycle and extension strategies
  • The product life cycle is the set of phases a product passes through from launch until it is withdrawn.
  • The phases in order are introduction (phase 1), growth (phase 2), maturity (phase 3) and decline (phase 4).
  • Sales are low at introduction, rise quickly in growth, level off in maturity and fall in decline.
  • An extension strategy is an action taken to stop sales falling and make a product last longer, usually used in maturity.
  • Examples of extension strategies: new packaging, new features or flavours, lower prices, new markets or customers, and more advertising.
  • Star, cash cow, question mark and dog belong to the Boston matrix, not the product life cycle.

Open the lesson and practice

Branding and why it matters
  • A brand is a name, logo, symbol or design that identifies a business's products and makes them different from competitors' products.
  • Branding helps customers recognise the product quickly.
  • A strong brand builds customer loyalty: customers keep choosing it.
  • A strong brand can allow a higher price because customers trust its quality.
  • A well-known brand makes promoting new products easier and more likely to succeed.
  • A brand can become very valuable to a business over time.

Open the lesson and practice

Boston matrix and the product portfolio
  • A product portfolio is the full range of products a business sells.
  • The Boston matrix sorts products using two measures: market growth (is the market getting bigger?) and market share (how much of it the product has).
  • Star: high market growth, high market share. Usually worth investing in.
  • Cash cow: low market growth, high market share. Brings in steady profit that can fund other products.
  • Question mark (problem child): high market growth, low market share. Uncertain; may need investment to succeed.
  • Dog: low market growth, low market share. Often dropped or replaced. A balanced portfolio has products in several categories.

Open the lesson and practice

Market research methods: primary and secondary
  • Market research means collecting information about customers, competitors and the market to help a business make decisions.
  • Primary (field) research is new information the business collects itself for its own purpose: surveys, questionnaires, focus groups, observation and test marketing.
  • Secondary (desk) research uses information that already exists, collected by someone else: the internet, market reports and government reports.
  • Primary research is up to date and specific to the business, but it usually costs more and takes longer.
  • Secondary research is quicker and cheaper, but it may be out of date, not exactly what the business needs, and also available to competitors.
  • A focus group is a small group of people who discuss a product in depth; test marketing means selling a product in one small area before a full launch.

Open the lesson and practice

Market segmentation and targeting
  • A market segment is a group of customers who share similar characteristics.
  • Market segmentation means dividing a market into these groups.
  • Common bases: location, age, income, lifestyle and demographics (characteristics of the population such as age, gender and family type).
  • Targeting means choosing which segment(s) to aim products and promotion at.
  • Segmentation lets a business design a product, price and promotion that fit one group's needs.
  • It avoids wasting money promoting to people who are unlikely to buy.

Open the lesson and practice

Place: distribution channels and e-commerce
  • Place is the part of the marketing mix about how and where customers can buy the product.
  • A distribution channel is the route a product takes from the producer to the customer, e.g. producer to retailer to customer.
  • A retailer is a shop that sells to the final customer; using other retailers lets a producer reach more customers.
  • An e-tailer sells online; e-commerce means buying and selling using the internet, websites or apps.
  • E-commerce can lower costs (fewer shops) and reach customers anywhere, at any time.
  • Concerns about e-commerce or booking apps: set-up and maintenance cost, customers unwilling to download apps, and needing technical skills.

Open the lesson and practice

Promotion methods: above and below the line
  • Promotion means communicating with customers to make them aware of a product and persuade them to buy it.
  • Above the line promotion uses mass media to reach a large audience: television, radio, newspapers, posters and billboards.
  • Below the line promotion is more direct and targeted: special offers, coupons, product trials and free samples, sponsorship, leaflets, loyalty schemes.
  • Sponsorship means paying towards an event or organisation so that the business's name is shown, raising brand awareness.
  • Product trials let customers try a product before buying, and give the business feedback to fix problems before a wider launch.
  • Promotion costs money; the right method depends on the target customers, the budget and the message.

Open the lesson and practice

Public relations and company image
  • Public relations (PR) means building and keeping a good image of a business and its products in the eyes of the public.
  • PR activities include charity donations, community projects, sponsoring local events, environmental actions and press releases.
  • A good image makes customers trust the business and more willing to buy from it.
  • Good PR can attract investors and staff who want to be linked to a respected business.
  • Bad publicity, such as faulty products or harm to the environment, can damage the image and reduce sales.
  • PR activities cost money, and there is no guarantee they will lead to more sales.

Open the lesson and practice

Technology in promotion and social media
  • Targeted online advertising shows adverts only to people likely to be interested, based on their searches, purchases or profile.
  • Viral advertising is content shared quickly from person to person on social media.
  • An e-newsletter is information a business sends electronically to its customers, often with new products and offers.
  • Digital promotion can reach many people worldwide, at any time, often more cheaply than traditional methods.
  • Customers use social media to post reviews: good reviews attract customers, bad or untrue reviews can drive them away.
  • Not everyone uses social media, so some customers may never see online promotion.

Open the lesson and practice

Why businesses carry out market research
  • The purpose of market research is the reason a business does it, not the method it uses.
  • Research helps a business identify and understand customer needs and wants.
  • Research can reveal a gap in the market: a need that no business is meeting yet.
  • Research reduces risk: the business is less likely to spend money on a product nobody wants.
  • Research findings inform decisions about the product, its price, where it is sold and how it is promoted.
  • Product trials and test marketing are research used to check a new product before a full launch.

Open the lesson and practice

Why marketing matters: needs, loyalty and share
  • Marketing is identifying what customers want and making sure the business provides it at a profit.
  • Satisfying customer needs leads to repeat purchases and word-of-mouth recommendations.
  • Customer loyalty means customers keep buying from the same business instead of rivals; loyalty cards and offers encourage it.
  • Market orientation: the business finds out what customers want first, then makes it. Product orientation: the business makes what it is good at, then tries to sell it.
  • Market share is the percentage of all sales in a market made by one business; high share often brings loyal customers and lower costs per unit.
  • Poor customer service damages the brand and drives customers to competitors.

Open the lesson and practice

Market research data: types and reliability
  • Quantitative data is information in the form of numbers, such as how many people would buy a product or what price they would pay.
  • Qualitative data is information about opinions, attitudes, feelings and reasons, such as why customers like a product.
  • Quantitative data is easy to compare and put in charts; qualitative data gives deeper reasons but is harder to analyse.
  • Social media lets a business collect data quickly and cheaply from large numbers of people through polls, comments, likes and reviews.
  • Reliable data is accurate and trustworthy; unreliable data (small or biased sample, dishonest answers, out of date) can lead to poor decisions.
  • Better data helps a business set prices, design products and target customers more accurately.

Open the lesson and practice

Pricing strategies and when to use them
  • A pricing strategy is the method a business uses to decide the price of a product.
  • Cost plus: work out the cost of making the product and add a markup (an extra amount) for profit.
  • Penetration: set a low price for a new product to gain market share quickly, then raise it later.
  • Skimming: set a high price for a new or unique product, then lower it over time.
  • Competition pricing: set a price similar to rivals' prices.
  • Promotional pricing: lower the price for a short time to boost sales, e.g. a discount or special offer.

Open the lesson and practice

Responding to changes in the market
  • Markets change: customer needs and tastes change, spending patterns change, and new competitors arrive.
  • A spending pattern is how much customers spend and what they spend it on; it rises or falls with income and trends.
  • Increased competition gives customers more choice, so a business can lose customers and market share.
  • Responses include new or improved products, price changes, more promotion, longer opening hours or new services.
  • Responding keeps a business competitive and protects sales; not responding can lead to falling sales and even failure.
  • Responding costs money and may not work, and a business with a strong unique image may need only small changes.

Open the lesson and practice

New products and marketing mix choices
  • The marketing mix is the four Ps: product, price, place and promotion.
  • A good is a physical item you can touch (a car); a service is an activity done for you (car washing, advice).
  • Businesses keep introducing new products to keep customers interested, keep up with trends and replace products that are declining.
  • A new product or feature can give a unique selling point (USP): something that makes it different from rivals.
  • New products cost money to develop and may fail, so research and careful planning reduce the risk.
  • Growth options in the case usually change one part of the mix: a new product or service, a price offer, or more promotion.

Open the lesson and practice

5 Business operations

Capital-intensive and labour-intensive production
  • Capital in production means machines, equipment and technology.
  • Labour means the people (workers) who do the work.
  • Capital-intensive production uses more machinery than labour, e.g. a car assembly plant with robots.
  • Labour-intensive production uses more workers than machinery, e.g. a hairdresser, chef or fruit picker.
  • Job production is usually labour intensive; flow production is usually capital intensive.
  • Kaizen and just-in-time are lean production methods, not types of capital or labour intensity.

Open the lesson and practice

Economies and diseconomies of scale
  • Average cost (unit cost) is the total cost divided by the number of units made: the cost of making one item.
  • Economies of scale: as a business grows and makes more, its average cost per unit falls.
  • Internal economies come from inside the firm, e.g. buying materials in bulk at a discount (purchasing) or borrowing more cheaply (financial).
  • External economies come from outside the firm, e.g. a whole industry growing in one area so skilled workers and suppliers are close by.
  • Diseconomies of scale: when a business grows too big, its average cost per unit starts to rise.
  • Common causes of diseconomies: poor communication across many layers or countries, slow decisions and paperwork (bureaucracy), and workers who feel unimportant and lose motivation.

Open the lesson and practice

Technology and robotics in production
  • Robotics means using robots, programmed machines, to carry out production tasks without people doing them.
  • Productivity is the output produced per worker or per hour; technology usually raises it.
  • Robots are accurate and repeat tasks identically, so there are fewer errors and quality is more consistent.
  • Robots can work 24 hours a day without breaks, so more is produced and cost per unit falls.
  • Other technology, such as ordering apps or computer systems, can speed up processes and free staff for other tasks.
  • Drawbacks: high buying and maintenance costs, fewer jobs for workers, and less flexibility than people for one-off tasks.
  • Firms must balance cost, productivity, quality and flexibility when choosing technology.

Open the lesson and practice

Lean production: JIT, Kaizen and using resources well
  • Lean production means making goods with as little waste as possible (waste of materials, time, space and money).
  • Inventory (stock) is the materials and finished goods a business holds.
  • Just-in-time (JIT): materials arrive or goods are made only when they are needed, so very little inventory is held.
  • JIT benefits: less storage space and cost, less damaged or out-of-date stock, less money tied up. Risk: a late delivery can stop production.
  • Kaizen means continuous improvement: all employees regularly suggest small changes to improve how work is done.
  • Kaizen benefits: better quality, less waste, lower costs, involved staff. Risk: depends on workers being willing to take part and takes time.
  • Using resources effectively (materials, machines, packaging, staff) cuts waste and costs and can help the environment.

Open the lesson and practice

Production methods: job, batch and flow
  • Job production: one item is made and finished at a time, often to a customer's own design (e.g. a wedding cake).
  • Batch production: a set number of identical items is made together, then the equipment is changed to make a different batch (e.g. a tray of one type of sandwich, then another).
  • Flow production: identical items move continuously along a production line in very large numbers (e.g. smartphones).
  • Job: unique products and motivated, skilled workers, but slow and costly per item.
  • Batch: some variety at a moderate cost, but time is lost and costs rise when equipment is changed between batches.
  • Flow: very low cost per unit and high output, but expensive to set up, inflexible, and repetitive work can bore employees.
  • Unit cost is the cost of making one item; it falls as more identical items are made.

Open the lesson and practice

Quality, quality control and competitive advantage
  • Quality means a good or service meets the standard customers expect and does its job well.
  • Quality control: checking products (often at the end of production) against a set standard so faulty items are found before reaching customers.
  • Total quality management (TQM): every employee, at every stage, is responsible for quality, usually supported by training and teamwork.
  • Quality in services means things like helpful staff, good facilities and reliable delivery of what was promised.
  • Benefits of quality: good reputation, repeat customers, recommendations, fewer returns and repairs, and the ability to charge higher prices.
  • Costs of quality: inspections, training and better materials cost money and take time.
  • Competitive advantage: something that makes customers choose one business over its rivals, e.g. higher quality, lower prices, wider range or a trusted brand.

Open the lesson and practice

Calculations

Average (mean) per unit or outlet
  • The mean (average) = total ÷ number of items (shops, showrooms, units).
  • Write large numbers in full before dividing: 9 million = 9 000 000.
  • The question may include extra figures (employees, turnover); use only the total and the count it asks about.
  • Counts of things are given as whole numbers.

Open the lesson and practice

Calculate the current ratio
  • Current assets are things the business owns that will turn into cash within a year: cash, money owed by customers (receivables) and inventory.
  • Current liabilities are debts the business must pay within a year, such as money owed to suppliers and overdrafts.
  • Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities, written as x:1 (for example 1.75:1).
  • A ratio below 1 means current assets would not cover short-term debts.
  • Pearson provides the ratio formulae in the exam, but you must know which figures go where and how to round.
  • Usually round to 2 decimal places; follow the instruction exactly if a different rounding is asked for.

Open the lesson and practice

Labour productivity: output per hour
  • Productivity measures how efficiently inputs are turned into output.
  • Labour productivity = total output ÷ labour input (hours worked or number of workers).
  • The unit matters: 'smartphones per hour' or 'units per worker'.
  • Higher productivity means more output from the same labour, which can lower the cost per unit.

Open the lesson and practice

Calculate the gross profit margin
  • Gross profit = revenue (sales) − cost of sales. Cost of sales is the direct cost of making or buying the goods sold.
  • Gross profit margin = gross profit ÷ revenue × 100.
  • It shows how many cents of gross profit the business keeps from each unit of currency of sales.
  • Pearson provides the ratio formulae in the exam, but you must know which figures go where and how to round.
  • Usually give the answer as a percentage to 2 decimal places.
  • Figures in thousands or millions are fine as long as both figures use the same unit.

Open the lesson and practice

One part as a percentage of a total
  • Percentage share = part ÷ total × 100.
  • Market share is one business's sales as a percentage of the total sales of all businesses in the market.
  • If the total is not given, add up all the parts first.
  • Both figures must be in the same unit (for example both in £ millions).
  • Give the answer to the decimal places asked, usually 2.

Open the lesson and practice

Total cost from fixed and variable costs
  • Fixed costs do not change when output changes, for example rent or a manager's salary.
  • Variable costs change with output, for example materials for each unit. They are usually given per unit or per hour.
  • Total variable cost = variable cost per unit × number of units (or per hour × number of hours).
  • Total cost = fixed costs + total variable cost.
  • The selling price is not a cost. Leave it out of a total cost calculation.
  • Keep the same time period (per day, per week, per month) and give the answer in the currency shown, usually to 2 decimal places for money.

Open the lesson and practice

Total revenue or total pay: rate times quantity
  • Revenue is the money a business receives from selling its products: revenue = price per unit × quantity sold.
  • Remuneration is the total reward paid to employees. Total pay = pay rate × hours worked, or pay per employee × number of employees.
  • Check the time period. 'Five every month' for a year means 5 × 12 months.
  • If there are several groups (factories, shops), add the numbers in each group first, then multiply by the rate.
  • Use the selling price for revenue, not the variable cost or the fixed cost.
  • Give money to 2 decimal places when asked and write the currency.

Open the lesson and practice

Break-even output using contribution
  • Break-even is the level of output where total revenue equals total costs: no profit and no loss.
  • Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit. It is what each unit adds towards paying the fixed costs.
  • Break-even output = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit.
  • The answer is a number of units (shoes, people, tubs), not money. Break-even revenue would be break-even output × price.
  • If the answer is not a whole number, round UP, because selling part of a unit is not possible and rounding down would leave a small loss.
  • Use fixed costs for the same period as the question (per day, per month, per year).

Open the lesson and practice

Calculate the acid test ratio
  • Inventory (stock) is goods the business holds to sell. It may take time to sell, so it is not counted as quick cash.
  • Acid test ratio = (current assets − inventory) ÷ current liabilities, written as x:1.
  • It is a stricter test than the current ratio of whether the business can pay its short-term debts.
  • Pearson provides the ratio formulae in the exam, but you must know which figures go where and how to round.
  • Usually round to 2 decimal places.
  • Figures in millions or thousands are fine when all three use the same unit.

Open the lesson and practice

Change between two values, absolute or percentage
  • Absolute change = new value − old value, in the original unit (tonnes, dollars, customers).
  • Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100.
  • Always divide by the ORIGINAL (earlier) value, not the newer one.
  • A negative result is a decrease. If the question says 'percentage decrease', give the size of the fall as a positive percentage.
  • Questions usually ask for 2 decimal places. Keep full calculator figures until the last step.
  • Units in thousands or millions do not matter for a percentage, as long as both figures use the same unit.

Open the lesson and practice

Find a percentage of an amount
  • To find x% of an amount: amount × x ÷ 100 (for example 5% of £850 = 850 × 0.05).
  • Commission is pay based on a percentage of the value of sales made.
  • A bonus is an extra payment on top of normal pay, often a percentage of earnings.
  • Interest is the charge for borrowing money, usually a percentage of the loan or overdraft.
  • In this type the answer is the percentage amount itself (the commission, the interest, the discount), NOT the new total.
  • If several items or people are involved, find the full base first (price × number). Money answers normally go to 2 decimal places.

Open the lesson and practice

Net cash flow and closing balance
  • Cash inflows are money coming into the business (sales, other income). Cash outflows are money going out (wages, suppliers, rent).
  • Net cash flow = total cash inflows − total cash outflows. It can be negative; show this with a minus sign.
  • Closing balance = opening balance + net cash flow.
  • The closing balance of one month becomes the opening balance of the next month.
  • To find a missing figure, rearrange: cash outflow = total inflows − net cash flow.
  • A cash-flow forecast is a prediction of future cash in and out. Give answers in the currency shown, as whole numbers when the figures are whole.

Open the lesson and practice

Work out profit or loss
  • Profit = total revenue − total costs. If the answer is negative, the business has made a loss.
  • Total costs means every cost line added together: labour, materials, other costs, or fixed + variable costs.
  • Gross profit = revenue − cost of sales (the direct cost of the goods sold). Per unit: (price − cost per unit) × units sold.
  • Operating profit = gross profit − operating expenses (overheads such as rent and salaries).
  • Write 'profit' or 'loss' clearly and give the currency. Large figures are usually whole numbers.

Open the lesson and practice

Calculate the operating profit margin
  • Operating profit = gross profit − other operating expenses (overheads such as rent, salaries, advertising).
  • Operating profit margin = operating profit ÷ revenue × 100.
  • It is always lower than the gross profit margin because overheads have also been taken away.
  • Pearson provides the ratio formulae in the exam, but you must know which figures go where and how to round.
  • Usually give the answer to 2 decimal places as a percentage.
  • Figures in £000s or millions are fine when both figures use the same unit.

Open the lesson and practice

Convert prices between currencies with exchange rates
  • An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another. €1 = 5 MYR means one euro buys five Malaysian ringgits.
  • Find the currency that has the '1' in front of it. Going from that currency to the other one: multiply by the rate. Going the other way: divide by the rate.
  • Rates can be written either way round, for example €1 = £0.84 or 5.65 MYR = £1. Read which side has the 1 every time.
  • If the question gives a quantity (50 handbags, 4 weeks, 6 bicycles), find the total first: quantity × price per unit. You can convert before or after multiplying; the answer is the same.
  • Money answers are normally given to 2 decimal places. Follow any other instruction exactly, such as 'to the nearest euro'.
  • Always write the answer in the currency asked for, with its symbol or code (€, £, MYR, CNY).
  • Sense check: if one unit of the new currency is worth less (many of them for 1), your number should get bigger; if it is worth more, your number should get smaller.

Open the lesson and practice

Increase or decrease a value by a percentage
  • Increase by x%: new value = old value × (1 + x/100). A 3.5% rise means × 1.035.
  • Decrease by x%: new value = old value × (1 − x/100). A 12.5% discount means × 0.875.
  • Or in two steps: find x% of the value, then add it (increase) or subtract it (decrease).
  • A discount is a reduction in price. A tax added to cost, a pay rise, interest added to a loan and growth are increases.
  • If several items or people are bought, find the full total first, then apply the percentage once.
  • Money answers are usually to 2 decimal places. Counts of things (vehicles, litres) are whole numbers.

Open the lesson and practice

Return on capital employed (ROCE)
  • Capital employed is the total long-term money invested in the business (owners' capital plus long-term borrowing).
  • ROCE = operating profit ÷ capital employed × 100.
  • ROCE shows how much operating profit the business makes for each unit of currency invested. Higher is better.
  • Pearson provides the ratio formulae in the exam, but you must know which figures go where and how to round.
  • Ignore other lines in the figure, such as current assets and current liabilities.
  • Give the answer as a percentage, usually to 2 decimal places.

Open the lesson and practice

Markup: percentage, selling price or cost
  • Markup is the profit added on top of the cost, shown as a percentage of the cost.
  • Markup % = (selling price − cost) ÷ cost × 100 = profit per unit ÷ cost × 100.
  • Divide by the COST, not the selling price. Dividing by price gives a different measure (a margin).
  • Selling price from cost: cost × (1 + markup/100). Cost from selling price: price ÷ (1 + markup/100).
  • Markup can be over 100%, for example when the price is more than double the cost.
  • Pearson provides the ratio formulae in the exam, but you must know which figures go where and how to round. Usually give markup to 2 decimal places and money in the stated currency.
