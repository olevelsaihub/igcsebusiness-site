Business / Technique cards / Analyse in context (6 marks)

Pearson Edexcel · International GCSE Business · 4BS1

Analyse in context (6 marks)

Write a longer answer that opens up a business idea or issue and follows its effects step by step, always using the business in the case.

Command word: AnalyseUsually 6 marksAbout 6-7 minutes.

What Pearson asks

Write a longer answer that opens up a business idea or issue and follows its effects step by step, always using the business in the case.

How the marks are earned

Half the marks reward using your business knowledge for this particular firm (application to the case). The other half reward analysis: building chains of reasoning where each step leads to the next, ending in a result for the business. Higher levels need detailed case use all the way through and complete, logical chains. No conclusion is needed.

Method

  1. Underline the topic word and the business name in the question.

    Why: Every point must be about this idea and this business, not business in general.

  2. Pick two relevant points and find a fact, number or detail in the case for each.

    Why: Case details are what turn general knowledge into application marks.

  3. For each point, write a chain: point, then 'this means', then 'which leads to', then the effect on the business.

    Why: Linked steps of cause and effect are what analysis marks reward.

  4. Finish each chain with a clear impact such as sales, costs, profit, cash or reputation.

    Why: A chain that stops half-way only shows limited connections.

  5. Keep to about two well-developed paragraphs and do not add a final judgement.

    Why: Analyse has no evaluation marks, so depth matters more than a long list.

Answer frame

[Point] for [business]... For example, the case shows [detail]... This means that... which leads to... As a result, [business] will...

Common errors

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Writing about business in general without naming the firm or using any case detail.
  • Listing many points with one short sentence each instead of developing two.
  • Stopping a chain before saying how it affects the business (sales, costs, profit, cash).
  • Defining the term at length, which earns little in a 6-mark question.
  • Adding a 'however' and a conclusion, which uses time without earning extra marks.

Worked illustration

November 2020 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q3(d)

The complete original question is shown; the illustration answers part (d).

Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

One limitation is that a break-even chart treats every tennis racquet HRMW makes as if a customer buys it. In reality some racquets may stay in the warehouse as unsold stock, for example if demand falls after the main playing season. This means HRMW could think it has passed its break-even output when the money from sales has not really come in. As a result it might over-produce and run short of cash to pay its suppliers and workers.

A second limitation is that the chart draws costs and revenue as straight lines. If HRMW raises output it may need to pay its factory staff overtime, so the cost of making each racquet rises, or it may have to lower its price to sell the extra racquets to sports shops. Therefore the real break-even point would be higher than the chart suggests, so HRMW's plans for how many racquets to manufacture could be too optimistic and its profit lower than expected.

Why it earns the marks

Both limitations are tied to HRMW and its racquets (application). Each one is developed in linked steps, from the chart's simple assumption to what really happens, then to the effect on cash, cost or profit (analysis). Two full chains keep the answer at the top level.
