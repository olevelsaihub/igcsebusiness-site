Break-even: meaning, charts and usefulness

Break-even point, reading break-even charts, effects of price and cost changes, and the usefulness and limitations of break-even analysis.

Type 7 3 Business finance BUS-S3-20

★★★★☆ · 11 of 32 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Difficult.

Knowledge

  • Break-even is the level of output where total revenue equals total costs, so the business makes neither a profit nor a loss.
  • The break-even output is found with: fixed costs divided by (selling price minus variable cost per unit).
  • On a break-even chart, the break-even point is where the total revenue line crosses the total cost line; the fixed cost line is flat.
  • Above break-even output the business makes a profit; below it, a loss.
  • Higher fixed costs or variable costs raise the break-even point; a higher selling price lowers it.
  • Uses: shows how many units must be sold, lets managers ask 'what if' questions, and supports loan applications.
  • Limits: it assumes everything made is sold, costs and revenue rise in straight lines, and figures are only predictions.

Answer technique: Analyse in context (6 marks) · Define a business term (1 mark) · Explain: point plus two linked expansions (3 marks) · Answer a multiple-choice question

Method

  1. Locate break-even: where total revenue equals total costs. Why: Most recall items test only this point.
  2. For a change, decide whether it raises costs or lowers price (break-even rises) or the opposite (break-even falls). Why: Direction questions are answered by this rule.
  3. For usefulness, link what the chart shows (units needed, profit at a given output) to the decision in the case. Why: Applied uses earn the context marks.
  4. For limitations, pick an assumption (all sold, straight lines, predicted figures) and show how it could lead to a wrong decision. Why: The schemes credit limits developed into consequences.
  5. For 6-mark Analyse, follow the Analyse card, using the case's own product and any break-even figure given. Why: Top level needs context in every point.

Common mistakes

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Placing break-even where fixed costs meet revenue, or where fixed and variable costs are equal.
  • Adding instead of subtracting in the break-even formula.
  • Getting the direction wrong, e.g. saying a higher selling price raises break-even.
  • Defining break-even in an Explain question, which earns no marks.
  • Naming a limitation without showing how it could lead to a poor decision.

Worked explanation

  1. Command: Explain one limitation, 3 marks: one limitation plus two linked steps.
  2. Limitation: the chart assumes all output is sold.
  3. Link 1: in reality some products may not sell, e.g. if a rival brings out a better model.
  4. Link 2: actual revenue is lower than the chart shows, so the business may need more sales to break even and could make a poor decision.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

One limitation of a break-even chart is that it assumes every unit the business produces will be sold. In reality a business such as Samsung may not sell all the televisions it makes, for example if a competitor launches a cheaper model, so some stock is left unsold. This means its real revenue will be lower than the chart shows, so it may need to sell more than expected before it breaks even and could make decisions, such as how much to produce, based on figures that are too optimistic.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for identifying the limitation (assumes all output is sold). 1 mark for the first link (some products may go unsold, e.g. rival competition). 1 mark for the second link (revenue lower than shown, so plans based on the chart may be wrong).
