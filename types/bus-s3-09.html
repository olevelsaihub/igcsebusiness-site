Cash-flow forecasts: terms, uses and limits

Inflows, outflows, net cash flow and balances, plus why businesses forecast cash and the limitations of forecasts.

Type 5 3 Business finance BUS-S3-09

★★★☆☆ · 10 of 32 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

The question mentions a cash-flow forecast, inflows, outflows, net cash flow or opening/closing balance, and asks what a term means, why forecasting helps, or its limits.

Knowledge

  • A cash-flow forecast is a plan that estimates the money expected to come into and go out of a business each month in the future.
  • Cash inflows are money coming in (e.g. sales, loans); cash outflows are money going out (e.g. wages, rent, supplies).
  • Net cash flow is cash inflows minus cash outflows for a period; it is negative when outflows are bigger than inflows.
  • The opening balance is the cash at the start of a month; the closing balance is the cash at the end, and it becomes next month's opening balance.
  • Forecasts help a business spot months when cash may run short, so it can arrange finance such as an overdraft in time.
  • Banks often ask to see a forecast before lending money.
  • A forecast is only an estimate: if sales or costs turn out differently, the forecast will be wrong.

Before you begin

  • The meaning of cash and cash flow (BUS-S3-08)
  • Simple subtraction of money in minus money out
  • What an overdraft is

Specification: 3.2.1 · 3.2.2 · 3.5.3

Answer technique: Define a business term (1 mark) · Evaluate and reach a supported conclusion (12 marks) · Explain: point plus two linked expansions (3 marks) · Answer a multiple-choice question

Method

  1. Check the term: inflow, outflow, net cash flow, opening or closing balance. Why: Recall questions test exact meanings and the order of subtraction.
  2. Say what the forecast shows in advance: expected money in and money out by month. Why: Every use of a forecast comes from seeing the future position.
  3. Link it to an action: spotting a shortfall, arranging an overdraft, delaying spending, or showing a bank. Why: The development marks come from what the business does with the information.
  4. For limitations, remember it is an estimate and can be wrong if sales or costs change. Why: This is the main weakness the schemes credit.
  5. For a 12-mark Evaluate, follow the Evaluate card: uses of the forecast for this business, its limits, then a conclusion on how far it helps decisions. Why: Top marks need balance and a judgement tied to the case.

Common mistakes

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Swapping the order: writing outflow minus inflow, or opening balance minus closing balance, for net cash flow.
  • Saying a forecast guarantees success or is a legal requirement.
  • Confusing a cash-flow forecast with a profit figure or with debts owed to creditors.
  • Defining a cash-flow forecast when the question asks for a reason it is important, which earns nothing.
  • Forgetting that the figures are estimates when asked for a limitation.

Worked explanation

  1. Command: Explain one reason, 3 marks: one reason and two linked steps.
  2. Reason: the forecast shows in advance when cash may run short.
  3. Link 1: knowing early gives time to act, e.g. arrange an overdraft or delay spending.
  4. Link 2: result: bills and wages are still paid, so the business survives.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A cash-flow forecast lets a business see in advance the months when the cash going out is likely to be more than the cash coming in. Knowing this early gives the managers time to act, for example by arranging an overdraft with the bank or putting off a large purchase. As a result the business can keep paying its staff and suppliers during that month instead of running out of cash, which could otherwise force it to close.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for the reason (it shows future months with a cash shortage). 1 mark for the first link (time to arrange finance or delay spending). 1 mark for the second link (bills keep being paid, so the business survives).

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Evaluate · June 2023 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define: forecast of monthly cash inflows, outflows and closing balance.
  2. Chain 1: forecast shows when cash from sales (e.g. UK tractors at 14 000 000 rupees, about £135 922) arrives -> time investment in new features or specialist staff.
  3. Chain 2: shows months with cash shortages -> arrange overdraft or delay projects -> pay suppliers and staff on time; links to 'Rising through crisis'.
  4. Counter: only a prediction; exchange-rate changes alter UK sales; fuel or material price rises increase outflows.
  5. Judgement: very useful for a large firm with big projects, mainly short term; depends on accuracy and regular updating against actual figures.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A cash-flow forecast predicts the cash that will flow into and out of a business each month, and the closing balance at the end of each month.

First, M&M can use a forecast to plan investment. M&M makes cars, vans and tractors, and developing new models or adding new features to its tractors needs large amounts of cash. If the forecast shows strong inflows from sales, for example from tractors sold to the UK at 14 000 000 rupees each (about £135 922 at 103 rupees = £1), M&M can see when it will have enough cash to pay for new machinery or to employ specialist engineers. This means it can invest at the right time without running short of cash.

Second, a forecast warns M&M of future cash shortages. If outflows are predicted to be greater than inflows in some months, M&M can arrange an overdraft or delay a project before the problem arrives. This helps it keep paying suppliers and employees on time, which protects its reputation. Its aim of 'Rising through crisis' shows that planning for difficult times matters to the business: in the 1970s oil crisis it had to adapt its engines quickly, and a forecast helps it judge whether it can afford such changes. It also helps M&M plan spending on its social and environmental projects, such as planting trees and supporting schools.

However, any forecast is just an estimate of the future. M&M sells abroad, so changes in the exchange rate affect its cash. If the rupee becomes stronger against the pound, the same tractor costs more in pounds for UK buyers, so fewer may be sold and inflows will be lower than forecast. Rising fuel or steel prices could also increase outflows unexpectedly. If M&M relies on an inaccurate forecast, it might start a project and then run out of cash.

Overall, cash-flow forecasting is very useful for M&M, because a large business with big projects must know when cash will be available before it commits money. It is most useful for short-term planning, such as the next few months. However, its value depends on how accurate it is, which depends on stable exchange rates and costs. M&M should update the forecast regularly and compare it with actual cash flow so that it can react quickly when things change.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines a cash-flow forecast (AO1); applies M&M's products, UK tractor price and exchange rate, 'Rising through crisis', oil-crisis history (AO2); developed chains on investment timing and avoiding shortages (AO3); limits from exchange rates and costs, then a conditional judgement (AO4).
