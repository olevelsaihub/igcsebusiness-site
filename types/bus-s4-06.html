Pricing strategies and when to use them

Cost plus, penetration, competition, skimming and promotional pricing: meanings, when each fits, and choosing between them.

Type 14 4 Marketing BUS-S4-06

★★★★☆ · 14 of 32 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

The question names a pricing strategy (cost plus, penetration, skimming, competition, promotional) or asks which pricing option a business should choose.

See frequency evidence and how ratings work

Stars describe how often this type appeared in past papers; they are not a prediction. 17 past-paper parts belong to this type.

Main plan: A-Grade Plan · Product life cycle, pricing, promotion and mix choices

KnowledgeMethodWorked examplePracticeTeach it back

Knowledge

  • A pricing strategy is the method a business uses to decide the price of a product.
  • Cost plus: work out the cost of making the product and add a markup (an extra amount) for profit.
  • Penetration: set a low price for a new product to gain market share quickly, then raise it later.
  • Skimming: set a high price for a new or unique product, then lower it over time.
  • Competition pricing: set a price similar to rivals' prices.
  • Promotional pricing: lower the price for a short time to boost sales, e.g. a discount or special offer.

Before you begin

  • Cost, revenue and profit
  • Markup as an amount added to cost
  • Market share and competitors

Specification: 4.3.2

Answer technique: Define a business term (1 mark) · Explain: point plus two linked expansions (3 marks) · Justify a choice between two options (9 marks) · Answer a multiple-choice question · State or give a point from the case (1 mark)

Method

  1. Name the strategy and say in one phrase how the price is set. Why: Definitions and multiple-choice parts test this directly.
  2. Match it to the situation: new or established product, many or few competitors, strong brand or not. Why: Each strategy suits different conditions in the case.
  3. Link the price to sales volume and profit per unit: low price means more sales but less profit on each one; high price the reverse. Why: This trade-off is the heart of every developed answer.
  4. Add a limitation: costs may not be covered, customers may not buy, rivals may react. Why: Justify answers need a 'however' for each option.
  5. For a 9-mark Justify, follow the Justify card: develop both strategies with case facts, then choose one and say why it fits better. Why: A clear, supported choice is needed for the top marks.

How each command word is answered

Command wordTimes askedWhat to write
State3One reason linked to this business, e.g. competition pricing so customers do not switch to a rival airline.
multiple-choice3Skimming = high starting price; penetration = low starting price; competition = match rivals; cost plus = cost plus markup.
Define1Price skimming: a new product is launched at a high price, which is reduced over time.
Explain2One benefit or use of the strategy, then its effect on customers, then the result for sales, share or profit.
Justify8Weigh both pricing options using case facts (costs, rivals, brand, target customers), give a drawback of each, then decide.

Common mistakes

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Confusing skimming (high start) with penetration (low start).
  • Saying cost plus guarantees profit without noting that the products still have to sell.
  • Writing about promotion methods instead of price when asked about promotional pricing.
  • In Justify, using no case facts such as the business's costs, rivals or brand.
  • Explaining several strategies when the question asks about one.

Worked explanation

  1. Command: Explain one benefit, 3 marks.
  2. Recall penetration pricing: a low starting price for a new product.
  3. Benefit: helps the product gain a foothold and market share fast.
  4. Chain: customers try it instead of rivals, then the price can be raised once it is established.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Penetration pricing helps a new product to become established in the market quickly. Because the price is set lower than competitors' prices, customers are tempted to try the new product instead of the brands they usually buy, so it gains market share fast. Once customers are used to buying it, the business can raise the price gradually and earn more profit on each sale while keeping many of those customers.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for the benefit (product becomes established quickly). 1 mark for why (lower price than rivals tempts customers to switch). 1 mark for the result (price can rise later, so profit improves while customers stay).

Justify · November 2024 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Option 1 skimming: a high launch price for new home robots (cleaning, shopping, security, entertainment) gives a high margin.
  2. Chain: high margin recovers research and development costs and funds the aim of becoming a multinational in five years.
  3. Option 1 drawback: many new entrants (NIO, Zoox, Vention) may undercut Saha, a small 15-employee firm.
  4. Option 2 cost plus: mark-up on parts and assembly costs guarantees profit on each robot sold, but ignores what customers will pay.
  5. Judgement: choose one; depends on how unique the home robots are and whether seven-day customer service justifies a premium.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Saha should choose price skimming (Option 1), although this depends on how different its home robots are from rivals' products.

Option 1, price skimming, means setting a high price when the home robots are launched and lowering it over time. The robots are new and do many jobs, such as cleaning, shopping, security and entertainment, so early buyers who want the latest technology may pay a premium. A high price gives a high profit margin on each robot. This helps Saha recover the large research and development costs of designing a new range, and that money can fund its aim of becoming a multinational within five years. However, Saha is a small business that opened in 2020 with 15 employees, and many new businesses are entering the global robot market, including NIO, Zoox and Vention in Türkiye. If customers think the price is too high, they may buy a rival's cheaper robot, and Saha could lose market share.

Option 2, cost plus, means Saha adds up the cost of making each robot, such as parts and assembly, and adds a mark-up for profit. It is simple to work out, and every robot sold covers its costs, so Saha makes a profit on each sale. However, cost plus ignores what customers are willing to pay. If home buyers would happily pay more for a new robot, Saha misses revenue it needs for growth. Also, as a small firm Saha may have high unit costs, so a cost plus price could end up above competitors' prices without any extra benefit for the customer.

On balance, Saha should choose price skimming. It has ambitious growth plans, and a high launch price is the quickest way to recover development costs and fund expansion, while its seven-day customer service gives buyers a reason to pay more. However, this depends on the robots offering features rivals do not have; if competitors launch similar home robots at the same time, Saha would need a lower price to win customers.

Why it reaches the top level: Applies both methods to Saha (new home robots, 15 staff, rivals NIO/Zoox/Vention, multinational aim, seven-day service), builds chains for each (high margin to recover R&D and fund growth; mark-up to guaranteed profit per unit), weighs drawbacks of both and gives a justified choice with a 'depends on' condition. Meets the top AO2-AO4 descriptors.

Justify · June 2019 · Paper 2 · Q2(f)

  1. Option 1 cost plus: Tata adds a mark-up to the cost of the new vehicle, so every vehicle sold covers costs and earns profit.
  2. Option 1 drawback: ignores what buyers will pay; a cheaper rival vehicle could take sales, especially in India's competitive market.
  3. Option 2 promotional: a launch discount attracts buyers to try the new vehicle and builds early sales for a large, well-known maker.
  4. Option 2 drawback: lower profit per vehicle, and sales may drop when the price returns to normal.
  5. Link to case: Tata aims to produce vehicles people want and need, and is developing electric cars that may cost more to make.
  6. Judgement: choose one; depends on how competitive the new vehicle's segment is and how high its production costs are.

Justify · June 2021 · Paper 1 · Q2(f)

  1. Option 1 low introductory price for three months: encourages farm-shop shoppers and overnight guests to try NOF pop.
  2. Chain: trial leads to higher sales and repeat buying once customers like it; but profit per bag is low in the first three months.
  3. Option 2 same price as competitors: customers who trust the local organic farm may pay a normal price, protecting profit per bag.
  4. Option 2 drawback: NOF pop is a new brand of popcorn, so with no price advantage shoppers in local retailers may choose a familiar brand.
  5. Judgement: choose one; depends on how well known NOF is in local retailers compared with in its own farm shop.

Justify · November 2021 · Paper 2 · Q3(e)

  1. Option 1 competition pricing: in a very competitive market a price close to rivals' looks realistic, so Huawei's new smartphone stays in buyers' choice.
  2. Option 1 drawback: the price may not cover Huawei's development and production costs, so profit could suffer.
  3. Option 2 promotional pricing: a lower launch price persuades users of other brands to switch, growing market share.
  4. Option 2 drawback: sales may fall when the price returns to normal, and profit per phone is lower during the promotion.
  5. Use case point: Huawei must keep launching new products to keep up with competitors.
  6. Judgement: choose one; depends on how strong the new phone's features are compared with rivals'.

Justify · June 2022 · Paper 2 · Q2(f)

  1. Option 1 cost plus: simple to apply; LEGO adds a mark-up so each Princess Leia toy sold covers its costs and makes a profit.
  2. Option 1 drawback: ignores how much Star Wars fans would pay, so revenue could be lost.
  3. Option 2 skimming: high launch price for a new, popular Star Wars toy gives a high margin even on fewer sales.
  4. Option 2 drawback: a high price may put off parents and children, or they may wait for the price to fall.
  5. Use case facts: LEGO is one of the world's largest toy makers and constantly introduces new toys to keep customers buying.
  6. Judgement: choose one; depends on how strong demand is from collectors and fans for the new toy.

Justify · November 2023 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

  1. Option 1 competition pricing: teenagers and young adults compare prices, so prices similar to other clothing retailers improve the chance of sales.
  2. Option 1 drawback: AWG's natural, sustainable materials and small workshops may mean costs too high to match rivals.
  3. Option 2 cost plus: AWG adds a mark-up to its costs, so each item sold makes a profit, protecting a small business.
  4. Option 2 drawback: ignores what young customers will pay; the price may be too high for low-income teenagers, or revenue may be lost.
  5. Use case facts: environmental objective, award for design, one store plus online sales abroad.
  6. Judgement: choose one; depends on whether young customers will pay more for environmentally friendly clothing.

Justify · June 2024 · Paper 2 · Q2(f)

  1. Option 1 competition pricing: in a very competitive travel market, a Sri Lanka holiday priced like rivals' looks fair, so TUI's brand and 1,600 agencies can win bookings.
  2. Option 1 drawback: matching rivals may not cover TUI's costs of adding a new destination.
  3. Option 2 promotional pricing: a launch discount attracts customers away from other travel businesses and builds market share.
  4. Option 2 drawback: bookings may drop once the discount ends, and profit per holiday is lower.
  5. Use case facts: 27 million customers, well-known brand, extras such as car hire and day trips that can add revenue.
  6. Judgement: choose one; depends on how many rivals already offer Sri Lanka and how price-sensitive customers are.

Justify · November 2024 · Paper 2 · Q2(f)

  1. Option 1 competition pricing: matching Indian rivals' room prices keeps Premier Inn competitive while it builds its brand in India.
  2. Option 1 drawback: guests may still choose another hotel for its facilities, as price alone does not win bookings.
  3. Option 2 promotional pricing: e.g. a discount on a multi-night stay brings in more bookings and restaurant spending.
  4. Option 2 drawback: lower price per room means higher bookings may not raise profit, and demand may fall after the offer ends.
  5. Use case facts: 820+ hotels, 83,000 rooms, restaurants in most hotels, need to be competitive in India.
  6. Judgement: choose one; depends on whether Premier Inn is already known to Indian travellers.
