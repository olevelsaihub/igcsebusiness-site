Measuring business success

The measures a business uses to judge its success (revenue, profit, market share, growth, customer, employee and owner satisfaction), comparing results over time and with rivals, and the limits of relying on one measure.

Type 15 1 Business activity and influences on business BUS-S1-35

★★★★☆ · 11 of 32 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Difficult.

The question asks how a business can measure or judge its success, which factors to consider, why it tracks profit or satisfaction, or why it compares itself with rivals.

Knowledge

  • Success means how well a business is doing: compared with its own objectives, with its past results, and with its rivals.
  • Financial measures: revenue (money from sales), profit (revenue minus costs), market share (the business's sales as a percentage of all sales in its market) and growth (more sales, outlets, products or staff).
  • Non-financial measures: customer satisfaction (reviews, complaints, repeat visits), employee satisfaction (staff turnover, absence, surveys) and owner or shareholder satisfaction (dividends, goals met).
  • A single figure means little on its own; it becomes useful when compared over time, against the target set, or with competitors.
  • Every measure has limits: profit can rise while customers are unhappy, and satisfaction is harder to measure accurately than money.
  • Missing expected growth has costs: shareholders may be unhappy and invest elsewhere, and the business may have less money to expand and may lose customers to rivals.

Before you begin

  • What revenue, costs and profit are
  • What market share means
  • What motivates customers and employees
  • What an objective is (BUS-S1-01)

Learn these types first: Business objectives: financial, non-financial and social

Specification: 1.8.1 · 2.4.1 · 3.4.1 · 3.5.2

Answer technique: Analyse in context (6 marks) · Evaluate and reach a supported conclusion (12 marks) · Explain: point plus two linked expansions (3 marks) · Justify a choice between two options (9 marks) · State or give a point from the case (1 mark)

Method

  1. Choose a measure that fits the business and what its owner wants. Why: State answers need a measure written for the named business.
  2. Say what a rise or fall in that measure would show about the business. Why: The explanation marks are for what the figure tells the owner.
  3. Compare the figure: with last year, with the objective set, or with a named rival. Why: Comparison turns a number into a judgement of success.
  4. Link the result to a decision or consequence, such as reinvesting profit, keeping staff or changing prices. Why: Higher marks need the effect on this business, not just the measure.
  5. For Analyse, follow the Analyse card: two developed factors, each using case facts. Why: Levels reward connected reasoning applied to the business.
  6. For Justify or Evaluate, follow those cards: weigh a money measure against a satisfaction measure, then judge which gives the fuller picture here. Why: Judgement marks need both sides and a supported conclusion.

How each command word is answered

Command wordTimes askedWhat to write
State2Name one measure linked to the case, for example a rise in sales of one named product, or market share compared with named rivals.
Explain3Give one measure (or one disadvantage of missed growth), then two linked steps: what the figure shows and what the business or its owners do next.
Analyse4Two chains using case facts, such as profit year on year or staff satisfaction lowering recruitment costs, each ending in an effect on this business.
Justify1Follow the Justify card: compare the two measures (for example customer satisfaction versus profit) in context, choose one, and say why the other is weaker.
Evaluate3Weigh profit or revenue against non-financial measures such as satisfaction or growth, using case data, then judge whether one measure is enough.

Common mistakes

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Giving an objective such as 'to survive' when the question asks for a way to measure success.
  • Naming a measure with no link to the named business in State answers.
  • Listing three measures with no explanation, which earns only the first mark.
  • Treating profit alone as proof of success without considering customers, staff or growth.
  • Saying a business should compare with rivals without saying what it would do with the result.

Worked explanation

  1. Read the command: Explain one measure, 3 marks, so one point plus two linked steps (Explain card).
  2. Pick one measure: market share.
  3. First link: say what a rise or fall in market share shows about the business compared with rivals.
  4. Second link: say how managers use that result to make a decision.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

One measure is market share, which is the percentage of all sales in a market that a business makes. If its market share rises from one year to the next, the business is winning customers from its competitors, which shows it is performing well against them. The managers can then use this result to plan, for example keeping a popular product range, or changing prices or promotion if market share starts to fall.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for identifying a measure (market share). 1 mark for explaining what a change in it shows (winning or losing customers against rivals). 1 mark for the linked consequence (managers use it to make decisions). Total 3.

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Justify · June 2025 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q2(f)

Answer plan

  1. Option 1 customer satisfaction: shows how happy tourists are with guides, hotel bicycle delivery, free maps and locks.
  2. Chain: good reviews already online -> new tourists read them -> more bookings -> more revenue.
  3. Limit: only some customers post reviews, so the picture may be biased.
  4. Option 2 profit: shows revenue covers costs like eight guides' wages and bicycles -> financially viable; limit: seasonal tourist numbers make profit swing.
  5. Recommend customer satisfaction for a service business built on reviews; depends on collecting feedback from most customers and CCT not being short of cash.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Option 1, customer satisfaction, measures how happy customers are with the service. CCT already has good online reviews and ratings, and customers say the tours are extremely enjoyable and that the guides help them experience the true culture of China. CCT sells an experience, so satisfaction tells the owner how well the eight bilingual guides, the delivery of bicycles to hotels and the free maps and locks are working. Satisfied tourists write good reviews, new tourists read these reviews before booking, so more bookings follow and revenue rises over time. However, satisfaction is hard to measure accurately. Only some customers leave reviews, and the ones who do may not be typical, so the owner could get a misleading picture.

Option 2, profit, shows whether the revenue from tours is greater than costs such as wages for eight guides and the cost of bicycles. If CCT makes a profit it is financially viable and can afford to replace bicycles or hire more guides. However, profit is affected by things outside the owner's control, such as fewer tourists in some seasons, so a fall in profit in one quarter may not mean the business is doing badly. Profit also does not tell the owner why customers are happy or unhappy.

I recommend Option 1, customer satisfaction. CCT is a service business that depends on word of mouth and online reviews, so satisfied customers lead to future bookings and, in the end, to profit. It also tells the owner what to improve, for example a particular guide or route. However, this depends on CCT collecting feedback from most customers, for example a short survey after every tour, rather than relying only on the few who post reviews. If CCT is short of cash, profit should be checked as well, because a business that cannot cover its costs will not survive however happy its customers are.

Why it reaches the top level: Both options applied to CCT (reviews, eight bilingual guides, hotel delivery) (AO2); each option has a developed chain to bookings or viability (AO3); a limitation for each, a clear choice with reasons and a 'depends on' condition (AO4).

Evaluate · November 2020 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

  1. Name two success factors: revenue/sales growth and customer satisfaction (quality of products).
  2. Case evidence: family business since 1925, exports footballs, boxing gloves, hockey sticks worldwide, named a top-10 Indian business, 50+ awards for exporting and quality.
  3. Chain 1: high quality -> satisfied customers return and recommend -> higher export sales and revenue.
  4. Chain 2: comparing revenue with competitors shows relative success; Figure 3 current ratio about 1.19 (64 650 / 54 550) shows it can pay short-term debts.
  5. Counter: revenue is only part of success; shareholders may want dividends that conflict with expansion; keeping customers happy may mean lower prices and lower profit.
  6. Judgement: quality and reputation drive HRMW's success; depends on keeping quality while controlling costs and cash.

Evaluate · June 2021 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

  1. Name measures: profit of the farm shop and non-financial measures such as customer satisfaction and environmental aims.
  2. Case: farm shop sells local organic produce, also used for overnight guests; aims to recycle, avoid packaging, cut electricity.
  3. Chain 1: compare farm shop profit with accommodation profit -> see which area to invest in or improve.
  4. Chain 2: social aims (supporting local farmers, less packaging) -> measure by customer feedback and environmental targets met -> loyal customers.
  5. Counter: profit depends on outside factors like weather and harvests; supermarkets sell similar produce more cheaply.
  6. Judgement: use profit plus customer/environmental measures together; depends on NOF's main aim (ethical vs financial).

Evaluate · November 2024 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

  1. Define profit: revenue minus costs; shows whether each hotel covers its costs.
  2. Case: 820+ hotels, 83 000+ rooms in UK, UAE, India, first hotel in Germany, plans for more countries; restaurants in most hotels.
  3. Chain 1: compare profit per hotel -> decide which hotels to expand and whether new countries are working.
  4. Chain 2: profit funds expansion -> more hotels; liquidity check: current ratio about 1.76 (9 566 / 5 447).
  5. Counter: profit is hit by outside factors like travel restrictions; ignores market share and guest satisfaction which drive repeat stays.
  6. Judgement: profit is the key measure for a growing chain, but not the only one; depends on whether Premier Inn also wants market share and repeat guests.
