Boston matrix and the product portfolio

Stars, cash cows, question marks and dogs, and using the matrix to review and balance a product portfolio.

Type 4 4 Marketing BUS-S4-05

★★☆☆☆ · 7 of 32 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

The question names the Boston matrix, a portfolio, or the words star, cash cow, question mark or dog, or market growth with market share.

Knowledge

  • A product portfolio is the full range of products a business sells.
  • The Boston matrix sorts products using two measures: market growth (is the market getting bigger?) and market share (how much of it the product has).
  • Star: high market growth, high market share. Usually worth investing in.
  • Cash cow: low market growth, high market share. Brings in steady profit that can fund other products.
  • Question mark (problem child): high market growth, low market share. Uncertain; may need investment to succeed.
  • Dog: low market growth, low market share. Often dropped or replaced. A balanced portfolio has products in several categories.

Before you begin

  • The meaning of market share
  • The idea of market growth
  • The product life cycle (to avoid mixing up the terms)

Learn these types first: Product life cycle and extension strategies

Specification: 4.3.1

Answer technique: Analyse in context (6 marks) · Explain: point plus two linked expansions (3 marks) · Answer a multiple-choice question · State or give a point from the case (1 mark)

Method

  1. Place the product using two questions: is market growth high or low, and is market share high or low? Why: Every category is defined by these two measures.
  2. Name the category and its usual decision: invest in stars, use cash from cash cows, build or drop question marks, drop dogs. Why: Uses of the matrix are what State and Explain parts reward.
  3. Link the decision to the whole portfolio: money from cash cows funds new products so the range stays balanced. Why: Portfolio balance is the main benefit credited.
  4. Apply it to products in the case, e.g. a cafe dropping its weakest menu items. Why: State and Analyse parts need context.
  5. For a 6-mark Analyse, follow the Analyse card: two applied uses with chains to profit or growth. Why: Level 3 needs detailed application and logical chains.

State2One use for this business, e.g. spotting which of its products are dogs and taking them off sale.
Explain1One benefit, such as balancing the portfolio, then how it guides decisions, then the effect on future sales or income.
Analyse1Two uses applied to the business's actual products, each chained to a decision about investing, keeping or dropping them.

Common mistakes

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Swapping the cash cow and star descriptions or the question mark and dog descriptions.
  • Choosing an extension strategy or packaging as a part of the Boston matrix.
  • Describing the categories but not saying how the business would use them.
  • Giving a use that is not linked to the business's own products in State answers.
  • Treating the matrix as showing profit directly instead of share and growth.

Worked explanation

  1. Command: Explain one benefit, 3 marks.
  2. Benefit chosen: helps the business balance its product portfolio.
  3. Expand 1: shows where the business is weak, e.g. too many similar or ageing products.
  4. Expand 2: leads to action, such as using cash cow profits to launch new products.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

The Boston matrix helps a business keep a balanced product portfolio. By placing every product according to its market share and market growth, managers can see whether they rely too heavily on a few cash cows and have too few stars or question marks coming through to replace them. They can then use the profit from the cash cows to develop and launch new products before the older ones fall away, so the business keeps a steady income in the future.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for the benefit (balancing the portfolio). 1 mark for what the matrix reveals (too much reliance on some categories). 1 mark for the action and result (new products funded in time, steady future income).
