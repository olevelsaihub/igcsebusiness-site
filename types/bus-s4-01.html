Market research methods: primary and secondary

Primary methods (survey, questionnaire, focus group, observation, test marketing) and secondary sources (internet, market and government reports): examples, benefits, drawbacks and choosing a method.

Type 5 4 Marketing BUS-S4-01

★★★★★ · 27 of 32 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

The question names a research method (survey, questionnaire, focus group, internet, report), asks primary or secondary, or offers two research options to choose between.

Main plan: A-Grade Plan · Marketing and market research

KnowledgeMethodWorked examplePracticeTeach it back

Knowledge

  • Market research means collecting information about customers, competitors and the market to help a business make decisions.
  • Primary (field) research is new information the business collects itself for its own purpose: surveys, questionnaires, focus groups, observation and test marketing.
  • Secondary (desk) research uses information that already exists, collected by someone else: the internet, market reports and government reports.
  • Primary research is up to date and specific to the business, but it usually costs more and takes longer.
  • Secondary research is quicker and cheaper, but it may be out of date, not exactly what the business needs, and also available to competitors.
  • A focus group is a small group of people who discuss a product in depth; test marketing means selling a product in one small area before a full launch.

Before you begin

  • What a customer and a competitor are
  • Why a business needs information before making decisions
  • The difference between cost and time as resources

Specification: 4.1.1 · 4.1.2 · 4.1.3

Answer technique: Analyse in context (6 marks) · Evaluate and reach a supported conclusion (12 marks) · Explain: point plus two linked expansions (3 marks) · Justify a choice between two options (9 marks) · Answer a multiple-choice question · Outline: two linked points in context (2 marks) · State or give a point from the case (1 mark)

Method

  1. Decide whether each method is primary (new, collected by the business) or secondary (already exists). Why: Most multiple-choice and State marks test this one sorting skill.
  2. Recall one strength and one weakness of the method: cost, time, accuracy, how up to date, how specific. Why: Benefits and drawbacks are what Explain and Justify answers develop.
  3. Link the method to the business in the case: who would be asked, where, and about which product. Why: State, Outline and Justify marks need the case, not a general answer.
  4. For a 3-mark Explain, follow the Explain card: one point, then two linked expansions ending in a business result. Why: Listing several points without development earns only 1 mark.
  5. For a 9-mark Justify, follow the Justify card: develop both methods, add a 'however' to each, then choose one using case facts. Why: Top marks need balance and a supported decision.

How each command word is answered

Command wordTimes askedWhat to write
multiple-choice10Sort the options into primary and secondary first; remember primary is more up to date but dearer and slower.
State5Give one method named for this business, e.g. a questionnaire for its own customers about a named product.
Explain6One benefit or drawback of a named method, then two linked steps ending in cost, time, accuracy or better decisions.
Outline2One method or reason plus one linked point that uses the business in the case.
Analyse1Two methods, each applied to the business and followed by a chain showing how it helps the decision.
Justify7Compare both research options in the case, give a drawback of each, then choose and say why the chosen one fits better.
Evaluate2Weigh benefits against costs and limits of the research for this business, then reach a supported conclusion.

Common mistakes

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Calling internet research or government reports primary research (multiple-choice distractors test this mix-up).
  • Claiming primary research is cheaper or quicker than secondary research; the schemes treat it as dearer and slower.
  • Listing three benefits without explaining any, which is capped at 1 mark in Explain.
  • Giving a general method in State and Outline answers without linking it to the named business.
  • In Justify, describing both methods but never making a clear choice backed by the case.

Worked explanation

  1. Read the command: Explain one benefit, worth 3 marks, so one point plus two linked expansions.
  2. Recall that secondary research uses information that already exists.
  3. Pick one benefit only: it is cheaper than collecting new data.
  4. Build the chain: why it is cheaper, then what the business gains from the money saved.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

One benefit of secondary market research is that it is cheap. The business uses information that has already been collected by someone else, such as a government report or articles found on the internet, so it does not have to pay staff or an agency to design and carry out its own survey. The money saved can then be spent on other parts of the business, such as developing or promoting its products.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for the benefit (low cost). 1 mark for explaining why it is cheap (the information already exists, so no survey has to be paid for). 1 mark for the consequence (money saved can be used elsewhere in the business).

Justify · June 2022 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Option 1 questionnaires in shops: many parents, easy-to-compare data on price and features -> right stock.
  2. Limit 1: parents only; rushed shoppers in malls and the airport -> poor accuracy.
  3. Option 2 focus groups: watch four-year-olds play; parents explain choices -> real user reactions -> toys that sell.
  4. Limit 2: costly, small samples, children act differently in a new room.
  5. Choose focus groups for a toy range for four-year-olds judged on play value; depends on getting a varied group of families.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Option 1, questionnaires handed out in the shops, is primary research. TBTS could ask many parents visiting its three shops what they look for in a toy for four-year-olds, such as price, safety or learning value. Because every customer answers the same questions, the results are easy to count and compare, giving quantitative data from a large sample at low cost. This would help TBTS decide which toys to stock and at what price, reducing the risk of buying stock that does not sell.

However, questionnaires reach only parents, not the four-year-olds who will play with the toys. Many shoppers are also short of time: two shops are in busy malls and one is at an airport, where travellers may be rushing for a flight. People may refuse or answer quickly without thinking, so the data could be inaccurate.

Option 2, focus groups with parents and their children, is also primary research but gives qualitative data. TBTS could let children play with sample toys while staff watch which ones hold their attention, and parents could discuss why they would or would not buy them. This shows real reactions from the actual users, not just what adults guess. TBTS could then choose toys that children enjoy and parents value, leading to higher sales and fewer unsold toys.

However, focus groups cost more and take longer to organise, and they involve only a small number of families, so the results may not represent all customers. Young children may also behave differently in an unfamiliar room.

I recommend Option 2, focus groups. The new range is for four-year-olds, who cannot complete a questionnaire, and TBTS selects toys for play, value, design and quality, which are best judged by watching children actually play. This gives deeper and more useful information for a new product launch. This depends on TBTS gathering enough families from different backgrounds for the results to be reliable. If time or budget is tight, a short in-shop questionnaire could then check the focus group findings with more customers.

Why it reaches the top level: Both methods applied to TBTS (three shops, malls and airport, four-year-olds, toys chosen for play/value/design/quality); chains from data type to stock decisions and sales; limitation for each; clear, case-based recommendation with a 'depends on' condition. Meets Level 3 for AO2, AO3 and AO4.

Evaluate · June 2024 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define primary and secondary research with examples.
  2. Primary: short questions to customers at 20 seats -> menu and hours that match needs -> loyalty among many rival cafés.
  3. Secondary: 5/5 reviews and rivals' menus -> free, quick view of strengths and gaps.
  4. Counter: small staff, time cost, rushed answers; secondary may be outdated; already successful (operating profit €84,054) and Kumaran is experienced.
  5. Judge: important but should be low-cost; depends on how fast the local café market changes.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Market research is collecting information about customers, competitors and the market to help a business make decisions. Primary research gathers new information first-hand, for example questionnaires, interviews or observation. Secondary research uses information that already exists, such as websites, online reviews and published reports.

Primary research is important for Café Pinch because it gives information about its own customers. Kumaran could hand short questionnaires to customers at the café's 20 seats or chat to them while they wait. He could ask which new meals or drinks they would like, or whether they want the café open later than 5pm. Because the answers come from people who already use the café, they are specific and up to date. Kumaran can then adjust the menu to match customer needs, which keeps customers returning and protects sales in an area surrounded by many other cafés.

Secondary research is also useful. Café Pinch already has 5 out of 5 online reviews, which show that customers value its high-quality cooked food, filled Ciabattas, range of drinks and customer service. Reading reviews is free and quick. Kumaran can also study the websites and menus of nearby cafés to compare prices and spot gaps, such as a product no rival offers. This helps Café Pinch stay competitive without spending much money.

However, market research has limits. Primary research takes time, and as a small café Kumaran may not have spare staff to run surveys during busy periods; customers may also refuse or give rushed answers. Secondary information may be out of date or not about Café Pinch's own customers. Most importantly, Café Pinch is already very successful, with top ratings and an operating profit of €84,054, so Kumaran's own experience from good Berlin restaurants and his daily contact with customers already tell him a lot.

Overall, market research is important but does not need to be costly for Café Pinch. Because the café is small, low-cost methods are the most suitable: checking online reviews and competitors, and asking regular customers short questions. These help Kumaran spot changes in customer needs before rivals do. How important research becomes depends on how quickly the local market changes; if many new cafés open nearby or Kumaran plans new products, more structured primary research becomes much more important to reduce the risk of a wrong decision.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines primary and secondary research accurately; applies to Café Pinch (20 seats, 8am-5pm, 5/5 reviews, many rival cafés, €84,054 operating profit, Kumaran's experience); developed chains to customer needs, loyalty and competitiveness; weighs limits; supported judgement with a 'depends on' condition. Meets Level 3 on AO1-AO4.

  2. Limit 1: costly and slow; low response; answers may be biased.
  3. Option 2 secondary (internet, reports): cheap, quick picture of the area, local competitors and suppliers.
  4. Limit 2: not about Lavender's own products; may be out of date or irrelevant.
  5. Judge: primary, because needs of customers in new areas are specific to Lavender's several brands; depends on budget and time; secondary can scope areas first.

  5. Judge: primary for promoting new electric cars, as Tata can afford it and its aim is to make what the public wants; depends on how new the product is.

  4. Limit 2: small groups may not represent all classes and routes; costly to run.
  5. Judge: questionnaires for a huge global passenger base; depends on whether Emirates needs breadth (questionnaires) or deeper reasons (follow-up focus groups).

  2. Limit 1: customers may not know about future trends; only those who visit the shop.

  1. Option 1 internet: read many up-to-date online reviews on hotels and cruises across 180+ destinations -> quick, cheap, wide picture.
  2. Limit 1: lots of irrelevant or unverified comments; time to sort them.
  3. Option 2 customer questionnaires: detailed, specific answers from TUI's own 27 million customers about hotels, cruises and day trips -> targeted improvements.
  4. Limit 2: many customers ignore questionnaires; slower to collect and analyse.
  5. Judge: depends on whether TUI needs speed (internet) or detail on its own service (questionnaire); link to aim of keeping its quality reputation.

  2. Importance 1: ask shoppers in the busy shopping centre what styles and prices they want -> right stock, compete with nearby shoe shops.
  3. Importance 2: useful before a new market (currently ladies only; margin on children's shoes 800 Ks on 3,200 Ks = 25%) -> check demand first.
