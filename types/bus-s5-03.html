Knowledge card

Production methods: job, batch and flow

Written

Job makes one item at a time, batch makes a set number then changes over, and flow makes identical items non-stop. The more identical items, the lower the unit cost.

Written-answer check
After your attempt, see the full worked explanation

Worked explanation

  1. Command is Explain for 3 marks: one reason, then two linked developments.
  2. Pick the key strength of batch: the business can make several different versions of a product.
  3. First link: the equipment can be switched to another size or colour after each batch.
  4. Second link: this gives customers variety without the high cost of making each item individually.
  5. Leave out any definition of batch production.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A business that sells many versions of a product, such as different sizes or colours, does not need huge numbers of any one version, so it makes each version in a smaller quantity. After one batch is finished the equipment can be reset to make the next size or colour. This lets the business offer customers plenty of choice without the high cost per item of making every product one at a time.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for the reason (a wide range of products, each needed in smaller numbers). 1 mark for the link that the product can be switched, e.g. by size or colour. 1 mark for the consequence that variety is offered without a large rise in cost. Total 3.

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Justify · June 2023 · Paper 1 · Q2(f)

Answer plan

  1. Option 1 batch: already used for chocolates -> range of shapes and flavours at low unit cost, competitive vs nearby shops.
  2. Limit: unsold batches if some flavours are unpopular; changeover time.
  3. Option 2 job: like special-occasion cakes -> personalised lollipops at a premium price.
  4. Limit: slow and costly per item; small cheap items, customers won't wait; only three employees.
  5. Recommend batch with reasons; depends on forecasting demand per flavour; could add job production for gifts.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Option 1, batch production, means making a set number of one type of lollipop, then changing the moulds or flavour to make the next batch. Irsi Chocolatier already uses batch production for its handmade chocolates, so staff already have the skills and equipment, and training costs are low. Making many lollipops at once lowers the cost per lollipop, so Irsi can offer a range of shapes and flavours at a competitive price, which matters because there are many other chocolate shops nearby. However, if demand for some shapes or flavours is low, Irsi may be left with unsold lollipops, and changing between batches takes time, which adds cost.

Option 2, job production, means each lollipop is made individually, often to a customer's own design. Irsi already uses job production for special-occasion cakes, so it could offer personalised lollipops, for example with a name or a special shape. These are unique, so Irsi could charge a higher price and stand out from nearby competitors. However, job production is slow and costly for each item. Lollipops are small, low-priced products, and most customers want to buy them quickly off the shelf rather than order and wait. Irsi has only three employees, so time spent on single lollipops would take them away from other work.

I recommend Option 1, batch production. Lollipops are a small, cheap product often bought on impulse, so customers want a variety ready to buy, and batch production gives this at a low cost per unit. It also uses the skills and processes Irsi already has for its chocolates. However, this depends on Irsi forecasting demand for each shape and flavour well so that batches are not wasted. If customers show strong demand for personalised lollipops as gifts, Irsi could offer job production as an extra service alongside its batch range, in the same way it offers special-occasion cakes.

Why it reaches the top level: Both methods applied to Irsi (batch for chocolates, job for cakes, three employees, many nearby rivals) (AO2); developed chains on cost per unit, range and premium pricing (AO3); a drawback for each and a clear choice with a 'depends on' condition and a fallback (AO4).

Justify · June 2019 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q2(f)

  1. Aim: keep bakery standards the same across 8 current and 8 new L.café outlets.
  2. Option 1 batch: make batches of different cakes -> can match local tastes in each area -> sales rise locally.
  3. Limit: less consistency between outlets; changeovers cost time.
  4. Option 2 flow: continuous production of identical cakes -> same standard everywhere, many cakes quickly, lower unit cost for 16 outlets.
  5. Limit: inflexible, cannot suit regional tastes; unsold cakes mean losses; high set-up cost.
  6. Recommend one (flow fits the 'same standard' aim) with reasons; depends on demand being high and similar across outlets.

Justify · June 2022 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

  1. Context: new product Dinky Deckers launched to keep customer loyalty; Cadbury is a major global maker within Mondelez.
  2. Option 1 batch: limited quantities -> can stop if unpopular; scarcity may raise interest.
  3. Limit: machine changeovers add cost and time; may run out if popular.
  4. Option 2 flow: large continuous output -> enough stock for all shops and supermarkets, low unit cost.
  5. Limit: if the bar is unpopular, Cadbury is left with large unsold stock.
  6. Recommend one; depends on how confident Cadbury is of demand (e.g. from trials) for a new, untested bar.
