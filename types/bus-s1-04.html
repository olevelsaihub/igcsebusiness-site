Pearson Edexcel · International GCSE Business · 4BS1

Forms of business ownership and limited liability

Sole traders, partnerships, private and public limited companies and public corporations: ownership, control, risk, limited liability, and the advantages, drawbacks and suitability of each form.

Type 5 1 Business activity and influences on business BUS-S1-04

★★★★★ · 22 of 32 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

The question names or describes a sole trader, partnership, Ltd, plc or public corporation, or asks about liability, owners' risk, or changing ownership form.

See frequency evidence and how ratings work

Stars describe how often this type appeared in past papers; they are not a prediction. 28 past-paper parts belong to this type.

Main plan: A-Grade Plan · Business activity, objectives, stakeholders and ownership

KnowledgeMethodWorked examplePracticeTeach it back

Knowledge

  • Sole trader: a business owned by one person, who keeps all the profit, has full control and has unlimited liability.
  • Partnership: owned by two or more partners who share decisions, skills, capital and profit; usually unlimited liability.
  • Private limited company (Ltd): owned by shareholders, shares are not sold to the public, owners have limited liability.
  • Public limited company (plc): shares can be bought and sold by the public on the stock exchange, so it can raise large amounts of capital.
  • Public corporation: a business owned and run by the government (the state), usually to provide a service rather than to maximise profit.
  • Unlimited liability: the owner can lose personal possessions to pay business debts. Limited liability: owners can only lose the money they invested.
  • A sole trader pays income tax on its profits; companies pay corporation tax.

Before you begin

  • What profit and debt are
  • What a share is
  • What capital (money invested) means

Specification: 1.2.1 · 1.2.2 · 1.6.1

Answer technique: Define a business term (1 mark) · Evaluate and reach a supported conclusion (12 marks) · Explain: point plus two linked expansions (3 marks) · Justify a choice between two options (9 marks) · Answer a multiple-choice question · Outline: two linked points in context (2 marks)

Method

  1. Identify the form of ownership in the question or case. Why: Each form has different owners, control, liability and finance.
  2. Recall its key features: who owns it, who decides, liability, and how it raises money. Why: A precise feature is the knowledge mark.
  3. Turn one feature into a benefit or drawback for the owner, then add its consequence. Why: Explain marks come from the effect on the owner or business.
  4. For Justify, follow the Justify card: compare both forms for this owner, then choose one and say why. Why: Judgement must use the case details, such as the owner's staff or plans.
  5. For Evaluate, follow the Evaluate card: weigh the gains of changing form against the costs, then conclude. Why: Top marks need both sides and a supported decision.

Common mistakes

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Mixing up a public corporation (government owned) with a public limited company (shareholders, stock exchange).
  • Saying a sole trader or partners have limited liability.
  • Writing a definition when the question asks for an advantage or disadvantage.
  • Listing three advantages with no explanation, which earns only one mark.
  • Thinking a private limited company keeps its accounts fully confidential; companies must publish financial records.

Worked explanation

  1. Read the command: Explain one advantage, 3 marks.
  2. Pick one feature of a sole trader: the owner has full control.
  3. Link it: no partners or shareholders to agree with.
  4. Add the consequence: decisions are quick, so the owner can react fast.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

One advantage of being a sole trader is that the owner has complete control over the business. Because there is only one owner, they can make every decision themselves without needing to agree with partners or shareholders. This means decisions can be made quickly, so the owner can respond fast to changes, such as stocking a new product that customers are asking for.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for the advantage (complete control). 1 mark for the link (no need to agree with anyone else). 1 mark for the consequence (quick decisions, fast response to customers). Total 3.

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Justify · June 2025 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Option 1 partnership: three of Gianni's 14 highly-qualified instructors bring fitness expertise and already know both gyms.
  2. Chain: part-owners are motivated and add capital, so service and revenue can improve (e.g. a booking website).
  3. Option 1 drawback: unlimited liability stays and control is shared with partners who may lack business experience.
  4. Option 2 Ltd: limited liability and a separate legal identity, so Gianni can invest in growth with less personal risk while choosing his shareholders.
  5. Option 2 drawback: set-up cost, paperwork, published accounts, and investors may not want shares in a small two-gym business.
  6. Judgement: choose one; it depends on whether Gianni can find suitable shareholders and how much control he wants to keep.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Gianni should choose a private limited company (Option 2), but this depends on whether he can find investors he trusts.

Option 1, a partnership with three of his employees, has real benefits. Four Fitness employs 14 highly-qualified instructors, so the three partners would bring fitness knowledge and already know how the gyms in Arezzo and Castiglion Fiorentino run. As part-owners they are likely to work harder, which could improve service for members and raise revenue. They could also add capital, for example to upgrade the website so members can book activities online. However, partners usually have unlimited liability. If the gyms ran into debt, Gianni could lose his personal possessions. He would also lose full control, because major decisions would be shared with people whose skills are in training, not in running a business, so disagreements could slow decisions.

Option 2 gives Gianni limited liability, so he could only lose the money he invested, not his home or savings. This matters because Four Fitness now has two gyms and 14 staff, so its costs and risks are far bigger than when it opened 20 years ago. With less personal risk, Gianni could invest with more confidence, for example in online booking or a third gym. The company would have a separate legal identity, so it could continue if anything happened to him. Shares are not sold to the public, so he can choose his shareholders and keep control by holding most shares. On the other hand, a company has set-up costs, more paperwork and must publish some accounts.

On balance, Option 2 is better because it protects Gianni's personal wealth while letting him keep control of a business he has built over 20 years; a partnership would share control and still leave him with unlimited liability. However, this depends on finding people willing to buy shares in a small, two-gym business. If he cannot, a partnership with trusted instructors may be the more realistic choice.

Why it reaches the top level: Applies both options to Four Fitness facts (two gyms, 14 instructors, 20 years, information-only website), develops chains for each (shared expertise to better service; limited liability to more confident investment), weighs drawbacks of both, and gives a clear choice with a 'depends on' condition about finding shareholders. Matches all three top-level AO2/AO3/AO4 descriptors.

Evaluate · November 2020 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define private limited company and limited liability; contrast with Ollie's unlimited liability as a sole trader.
  2. Case evidence: all his savings invested, heavy spending on computer technology, profits not reliable, 60-hour working week.
  3. Benefit chains: personal assets protected; shares sold to chosen people raise capital; shareholders share the workload, improving work-life balance.
  4. Drawbacks: set-up cost and paperwork, published accounts seen by rivals, dividends and loss of control, few investors for a small school.
  5. Compare with an alternative such as a bank loan that keeps full control but must be repaid with interest.
  6. Judgement: whether TADS should convert, depending on finding willing investors and Ollie's attitude to sharing control.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A private limited company (Ltd) is owned by shareholders whose shares are not sold to the public. Its owners have limited liability, so they can only lose the money they have invested.

There are strong reasons for TADS to become a private limited company. At the moment Ollie is a sole trader, so he has unlimited liability. He invested all of his savings in the drum school and has also invested heavily in computer technology, yet the business does not have reliable profits. If TADS made losses and could not pay its debts, Ollie could lose his home and other personal possessions. As a Ltd, TADS would have a separate legal identity, so his personal assets would be protected. Lower personal risk could make him more willing to expand as the school becomes more successful.

Becoming a Ltd could also help Ollie raise capital by selling shares to people he chooses, such as family, friends or local musicians who share his passion for drumming. This money could pay for more teachers or equipment. New shareholders could also share some of the work and decisions. This matters because Ollie works 60 hours a week and struggles with his work-life balance. Sharing the load could let him focus on teaching and on promotion, such as his newspaper columns and festival performances, which bring in new students and revenue.

However, there are drawbacks. Setting up a company costs money and involves more paperwork, and its accounts must be published, so rival drum schools could see how TADS is performing. Ollie would have to share profits as dividends and could lose some control if he sells too many shares. Also, TADS is a small school with unreliable profits, so few people may want to buy shares. Ollie might find a bank loan simpler, as it keeps him in full control, although a loan must be repaid with interest even when profits are low.

Overall, TADS should consider becoming a private limited company, because limited liability would protect Ollie's savings in a business with unreliable profits, and new shareholders could ease his 60-hour week. However, this depends on whether Ollie can find investors willing to buy shares in a small drum school, and whether he is happy to share control. If profits stay unreliable and nobody wants to invest, remaining a sole trader and using a bank loan may be better in the short term.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines Ltd and limited liability (AO1), applies them to TADS facts (all savings invested, 60-hour week, unreliable profits, columns and festivals) (AO2), builds chains to protected assets, new capital and better work-life balance (AO3), weighs paperwork, published accounts, loss of control and the bank-loan alternative, then judges with a clear 'depends on' (AO4).
