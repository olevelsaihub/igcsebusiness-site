New products and marketing mix choices

Goods versus services, the marketing mix, why businesses keep developing new products, and choosing between product, price or promotion growth options.

Type 16 4 Marketing BUS-S4-07

★★★★☆ · 11 of 32 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Difficult.

The question asks about the marketing mix, goods versus services, why firms launch new products, or offers two growth options such as a new course, new range or new feature.

KnowledgeMethodWorked examplePracticeTeach it back

Knowledge

  • The marketing mix is the four Ps: product, price, place and promotion.
  • A good is a physical item you can touch (a car); a service is an activity done for you (car washing, advice).
  • Businesses keep introducing new products to keep customers interested, keep up with trends and replace products that are declining.
  • A new product or feature can give a unique selling point (USP): something that makes it different from rivals.
  • New products cost money to develop and may fail, so research and careful planning reduce the risk.
  • Growth options in the case usually change one part of the mix: a new product or service, a price offer, or more promotion.

Before you begin

  • The four Ps
  • Revenue, costs and cash flow
  • Customer needs and competitors

Learn these types first: Product life cycle and extension strategies · Pricing strategies and when to use them · Promotion methods: above and below the line

Specification: 3.2.2 · 4.2.1 · 4.2.2 · 4.3.1 · 4.3.2 · 4.3.4

Answer technique: Explain: point plus two linked expansions (3 marks) · Justify a choice between two options (9 marks) · Answer a multiple-choice question · State or give a point from the case (1 mark)

Method

  1. Identify which part of the mix each option changes: product, price, place or promotion. Why: This tells you which benefits and risks to discuss.
  2. Link the option to customers: who it attracts (new or existing) and why they would buy. Why: This is the AO2 application in Justify answers.
  3. Carry the chain to revenue, market share or cash flow, using a fact from the case. Why: AO3 marks reward the consequence for the business.
  4. Give a drawback of each option: cost, time, risk that customers do not want it. Why: AO4 marks need a limitation for each side.
  5. For a 9-mark Justify, follow the Justify card: choose one option and explain why it fits this business better. Why: An unsupported or missing choice limits the mark.

How each command word is answered

Command wordTimes askedWhat to write
State1One benefit to the named business of its new product, e.g. it attracts customers who want that new feature.
multiple-choice2Goods are physical; services are activities. The four Ps are product, price, place and promotion.
Explain2One benefit of new products, then how customers react, then the effect on sales or keeping up with trends.
Justify6Develop both options with case facts and a drawback each, then choose; refer to cost, risk and likely revenue.

Common mistakes

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Picking a service such as gardening or financial advice as an example of a good.
  • Choosing production or profit as an element of the marketing mix.
  • Giving a definition of a new product instead of a benefit.
  • In Justify, praising one option and ignoring the other.
  • Ignoring costs and risk when recommending a new product or service.

Worked explanation

  1. Command: Explain one benefit, 3 marks.
  2. Benefit: keeps customers interested in the business.
  3. Expand 1: replaces old or out-of-date products, giving customers a reason to return.
  4. Expand 2: keeps up with trends, so customers do not switch to rivals and sales are maintained.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Continually introducing new products keeps customers interested in the business. When older products become out of date or customers become bored with them, a new product gives customers a reason to come back and buy again instead of switching to a competitor. This helps the business keep up with changing trends in the market, so its sales are maintained over time.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for the benefit (keeps customers interested). 1 mark for the link (new products replace old ones, so customers return). 1 mark for the result (keeps up with trends, so sales are maintained).

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Justify · June 2024 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q2(f)

Answer plan

  1. Option 1 vegan range (product): new segment, adapt existing menu at low cost -> daily sales, point of difference from new Berlin cafés.
  2. Limit 1: demand unknown; needs research; possible waste.
  3. Option 2 7.5% Wednesday 9am-12pm (price): fills quiet hours with paid staff; may convert to regulars.
  4. Limit 2: lower profit per sale; 20 seats limit extra customers; easy to copy.
  5. Choose vegan range; depends on research showing enough demand.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Option 1 changes the product element of the marketing mix by adding a vegan range. This lets Café Pinch reach a new market segment: customers who do not eat animal products and who may currently go elsewhere. Because Kumaran already cooks a range of hot and cold meals and filled Ciabattas, he could adapt existing recipes, keeping start-up costs fairly low. Attracting a new group of customers could raise sales on every day of the week and gives the café a point of difference from the growing number of cafés, small restaurants and breakfast bars in Berlin.

However, Kumaran does not yet know whether enough of his customers want vegan food. He would need to research demand and check what nearby competitors already sell. New ingredients could be wasted if the range does not sell.

Option 2 changes the price element with 7.5% off on Wednesday mornings, 9am–12pm. This could bring customers in at a time that may be quiet, filling empty seats while using staff who are already being paid. Customers who try the café because of the offer may come back at full price.

However, the discount cuts the profit on every item sold during those hours, including sales to regular customers who would have come anyway. With only 20 seats, Café Pinch cannot serve many extra customers at once, and long waits could damage its excellent service reputation. Rivals could also copy a price cut easily.

I recommend Option 1, the vegan range. It can attract new customers every day rather than for three hours a week, it builds on Kumaran's skill and high-quality food, and it is harder for competitors to copy than a discount. This depends on Kumaran first checking that there is enough demand for vegan food among his customers and the people using the nearby underground station. If research shows little demand, the Wednesday discount would be a lower-risk way to keep customers.

Why it reaches the top level: Uses marketing-mix terms (product, price, segment); applies Café Pinch facts (menu, Ciabattas, 20 seats, 7.5% Wednesday 9am-12pm, new Berlin rivals, underground station); chains to sales, profit and competitiveness; limitation for each; justified choice with a 'depends on' condition. Meets Level 3 for AO2, AO3 and AO4.

Justify · November 2020 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q3(e)

  1. Option 1 pop-up events across the country: Loaf already runs them in Birmingham -> know-how, sells bakery products to new customers -> cash inflow.
  2. Limit 1: Loaf is unknown outside Birmingham; travel, venue and staff costs with nine staff -> outflows may exceed inflows.
  3. Option 2 new cookery courses: uses existing kitchen and skilled chefs -> extra inflow with small extra outflow; fits aim of promoting real food.
  4. Limit 2: courses must run even if not full -> chef and food costs; demand uncertain.
  5. Judge on net cash flow: courses use existing resources so lower risk; depends on local demand for new courses.

Justify · November 2020 · Paper 1 · Q3(e)

  1. Option 1 teach other instruments: widens target market in wealthy Cricklade where TADS has no nearby rivals -> more students, revenue; matches objective to expand.
  2. Limit 1: must hire and pay other teachers; Ollie already works 60 hours a week.
  3. Option 2 more online drum lessons: uses existing computer technology; reach students anywhere -> revenue without new premises.
  4. Limit 2: technical problems could interrupt lessons and harm reputation; Ollie's time still limits lessons.
  5. Judge: depends on cost of extra teachers vs Ollie's limited time and unreliable profits; justify which raises revenue with less strain.

Justify · June 2022 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q2(f)

  1. Option 1 more advertising: reach tourists near popular attractions who do not know the 1786 restaurant -> more diners -> revenue.
  2. Limit 1: advertising costs money; many nearby restaurants also advertise; results not guaranteed.
  3. Option 2 opening for breakfast: currently lunch and dinner only -> new sales period using existing premises; something new in its long history.
  4. Limit 2: extra staff, food and energy costs; family workforce already stretched; tourists may not expect breakfast there.
  5. Judge: depends on whether premises are unused in mornings and staff availability; compare extra revenue to extra cost.

Justify · June 2023 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q2(f)

  1. Option 1 more promotion of all vehicles: more people aware of M&M cars, vans and tractors and its green image (1.32 million trees) -> showroom visits -> sales.
  2. Limit 1: costly; awareness does not guarantee purchase if products are not improved.
  3. Option 2 improve all vehicles: new features keep customers interested; fits 'Rising through crisis' and adapting history (diesel engines in the 1970s).
  4. Limit 2: R&D costs high; price rises may deter buyers; takes time.
  5. Judge: improvements give longer-lasting advantage for the world's largest maker; depends on M&M's ability to keep prices competitive.

Justify · June 2025 · Paper 1 · Q2(f)

  1. Option 1 Technogym Electronic Key within subscription: USP plus instructor analysis from 14 qualified instructors -> attract and keep members -> market share.
  2. Limit 1: cost of keys and instructor time; members who do not want it may feel overcharged.
  3. Option 2 paid online classes via website: website is information-only now -> new service for members at home and people beyond Arezzo and Castiglion Fiorentino.
  4. Limit 2: website must be upgraded; competes with free online videos; less social, while the gym values social activities.
  5. Judge: depends on whether Four Fitness wants to grow locally (key) or beyond its two towns (online); weigh set-up cost and customer demand.
