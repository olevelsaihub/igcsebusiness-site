Choosing where to locate a business

Factors in choosing or changing location: closeness to market, labour, materials and competitors, nature of the business, the internet, legal controls, and choosing between sites.

Type 12 1 Business activity and influences on business BUS-S1-12

★★★★☆ · 11 of 32 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

The question asks where a business should locate or relocate, why a location factor matters, or which of two sites to choose.

See frequency evidence and how ratings work

Stars describe how often this type appeared in past papers; they are not a prediction. 12 past-paper parts belong to this type.

Main plan: A-Grade Plan · Location, going global, currencies and interest rates

KnowledgeMethodWorked examplePracticeTeach it back

Knowledge

  • Location means where a business places its factory, shop, office or other premises.
  • Closeness to the market: being near customers increases sales and lowers delivery costs.
  • Closeness to labour: being near enough suitable workers, with the right skills, at an affordable wage.
  • Closeness to materials: being near suppliers lowers transport costs and avoids delays.
  • Competitors: some firms locate away from rivals; others cluster near them where customers already shop.
  • Other factors: the type of business, cost of land or rent, the internet (selling online means location matters less), laws and trading blocs.

Before you begin

  • What fixed and variable costs are
  • What a target market is

Specification: 1.4.1 · 4.3.3

Answer technique: Analyse in context (6 marks) · Evaluate and reach a supported conclusion (12 marks) · Explain: point plus two linked expansions (3 marks) · Justify a choice between two options (9 marks) · Outline: two linked points in context (2 marks) · State or give a point from the case (1 mark)

Method

  1. Decide what kind of business it is: one that serves customers in person, or one that makes goods. Why: This decides which factors matter most.
  2. Pick the factor that matters most for this business and use case evidence. Why: Context answers are only credited when they fit the named business.
  3. Link the factor to costs, sales or staff, and then to profit. Why: Explain and Analyse marks come from the effect of the factor.
  4. For Evaluate, follow the Evaluate card: weigh two or more factors against each other, then judge which matters most. Why: Judgement needs a supported conclusion.
  5. For Justify, follow the Justify card: compare both sites for this business, choose one and explain why. Why: The choice must be backed by the case.

How each command word is answered

Command wordTimes askedWhat to write
State1Name one factor that fits the case, such as being near qualified staff the business needs.
Outline2Give the reason, then link it to the workers or materials this business specifically needs.
Explain1One factor plus two linked steps, for example near materials, cheaper delivery, lower costs and higher profit.
Analyse3Two factors, each linked to costs, sales or staff for the named business.
Evaluate4Weigh several factors such as market, labour, materials and cost, decide which matters most for this business, and justify.
Justify1Compare both locations for the named business's customers and costs, choose one, and explain why it is better.

Common mistakes

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Giving a factor with no link to the named business in State and Outline answers.
  • Listing several factors without explaining how any one affects costs or sales.
  • Defining 'location' or 'proximity' instead of giving a reason.
  • In Evaluate answers, describing factors without deciding which matters most.

Worked explanation

  1. Read the command: Explain one reason, 3 marks; no definition needed.
  2. Pick one reason: lower transport costs.
  3. Link it: materials travel only a short distance.
  4. Finish: lower total costs and higher profit.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Being close to materials can lower the business's transport costs. Because the parts and materials it needs travel only a short distance from the supplier to the new factory, deliveries are cheaper. This lowers the total cost of making each product, such as each of Saha's robots, so the business can make more profit on each one or charge lower prices than rivals.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for the reason (lower transport costs). 1 mark for the link (materials travel a short distance, so delivery is cheaper). 1 mark for the consequence (lower total costs, higher profit). Total 3.

Grade 9 long answers

Evaluate · June 2019 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Factors: skilled labour (engineers, designers) and infrastructure (ports, roads, rail).
  2. Case: cars, buses, trucks, electric cars; centres in Korea, Italy, UK; 60,000 staff; 9 million car sales; 6,000 showrooms.
  3. Chain 1: skilled workforce nearby -> lower training/recruitment cost and high quality -> protects reputation and sales.
  4. Chain 2: good transport links -> parts in, vehicles out to showrooms cheaply and quickly -> lower cost per vehicle.
  5. Counter: skilled areas have higher wages and land prices; infrastructure may matter less than other factors such as market or government grants.
  6. Judgement: skills first for complex electric cars; depends on what the factory will produce and its budget.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Choosing a location means finding the place that gives the best balance between costs and revenue. For a vehicle maker like Tata Motor Company, two key factors are the availability of skilled labour and access to transport links (infrastructure).

Skilled labour: Tata makes cars, buses and trucks and is developing electric cars, so it needs engineers, designers and skilled assembly workers. If a new factory is close to a large pool of these workers, Tata spends less time and money on recruitment and training, and quality stays high. This protects Tata's reputation and helps it keep selling in large numbers; it sold 9 million cars in 2017. Tata already follows this approach with manufacturing centres in Korea, Italy and the UK.

Infrastructure: a car factory needs a steady supply of heavy parts such as steel and engines, and finished vehicles must reach Tata's 6,000 showrooms around the world. A site near ports, motorways or railways lowers transport costs and delivery times, so vehicles reach markets faster and the cost per vehicle falls, which increases profit.

Other factors include being close to markets where demand is growing and any government support, such as grants. Because Tata builds schools and houses and invests in the health of local people, it may also look for places where it can help the community, and local governments may welcome it.

However, these factors can conflict. Areas with many skilled workers are often developed regions where wages and land prices are high, which raises costs. A cheaper site may lack skilled labour, so Tata would have to spend more on training. Also, transport links matter less if the factory mainly sells in the country where it is built.

In my judgement, skilled labour is the most important factor, because electric cars are a complex, high-technology product, and quality problems would damage a brand that has been trading since 1868. With a turnover of $42 billion, Tata can afford higher wages or land costs. However, this depends on what the new factory will make: a factory producing simple truck parts in large volumes might be better placed where labour and land are cheapest and transport links strongest, while an electric car factory needs skills first.

Why it reaches the top level: Knowledge of labour and infrastructure factors (AO1); applied to Tata's vehicles, electric cars, 9 million sales, 6,000 showrooms, $42bn turnover (AO2); developed cost and quality chains (AO3); conflict between factors and a reasoned judgement that depends on the factory's product (AO4).

Justify · June 2025 · Paper 2 · Q3(e)

Answer plan

  1. Option 1 supermarkets: many shoppers -> coffee after shopping, add cake/sandwich -> higher spend per visit.
  2. Limit: shoppers may want to go home; supermarket cafés compete.
  3. Option 2 hospitals: visitors, patients, staff all day -> steady demand, few competitors; fits mission of nurturing people.
  4. Limit: visitors short of time; small, costly space.
  5. Recommend one (e.g. hospitals) with reasons; depends on hospital size/footfall, and when supermarkets would be better.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Option 1 is to open coffee shops inside supermarkets. Supermarkets have very large numbers of shoppers every day, so Starbucks could gain customers who want a drink and a rest after their food shopping. Because Starbucks also sells cakes, biscuits and sandwiches, shoppers may buy food as well as coffee, which raises the average spend per customer and the revenue of each shop. However, there is no promise that shoppers will stop; many want to take their shopping home quickly, and some supermarkets have their own cheaper cafés that would compete with Starbucks.

Option 2 is to open coffee shops in hospitals. Hospitals have visitors, patients and staff all day and often at night, and there are usually few other places nearby to buy a drink. Visitors who have travelled a long way may want a drink and something to eat, and they may even bring the patient they are visiting. This gives Starbucks steady demand with little competition. It also fits Starbucks' mission to nurture the human spirit one person at a time, because it offers comfort at a stressful time. However, visitors may have little time and may not want to spend it in a coffee shop, and hospital space may be small and costly to rent.

I recommend Option 2, hospitals. Demand is steady every day of the year and there are fewer competitors, so each shop has a reliable flow of customers. It also links strongly to Starbucks' mission and could improve its image. However, this depends on the size of the hospital: a large city hospital with many visitors and staff working long shifts would bring enough sales to cover the rent, but a small hospital might not. If Starbucks can find busy supermarkets that have no café of their own, Option 1 could bring higher sales.

Why it reaches the top level: Each option applied to Starbucks (food range, mission) (AO2); developed chains to spend per customer and steady demand (AO3); a limitation for each and a justified choice with a 'depends on' condition and when the other option would win (AO4).

Evaluate · November 2020 · Paper 1 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

  1. Factors: availability of skilled chefs/bakers and closeness to competitors (also local community need).
  2. Case: social enterprise, 9 staff, cookery courses, aims to restore the high street, 'Waste Not' donations, recycling hub.
  3. Chain 1: skilled chefs nearby -> no training costs -> money spent on charitable work and courses.
  4. Chain 2: location away from other bakeries -> less competition -> better chance of survival.
  5. Counter: no skilled labour -> new bakers lack skills; as a social enterprise Loaf may choose a struggling high street or be near competitors because its aims differ.
  6. Judgement: choose based on social aim plus skilled staff; depends on whether the second site's community needs Loaf's help and can support sales.

Evaluate · November 2021 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

  1. Factors: skilled labour (art-skilled staff) and availability of art materials; also customer demand.
  2. Case: second studio in small mining town Sungai Lembing; plans to recruit highly skilled employees; sells art materials; Liberty Art Jam, Corporate Art Jam, Kids Classes.
  3. Chain 1: skilled staff -> high-standard classes -> can offer the full course range like the Singapore studio.
  4. Chain 2: art supplies available -> same courses and material sales at both studios -> consistent service.
  5. Counter: small remote town may lack skilled staff and suppliers; fewer corporate customers than busy Kampong Glam; leaflets cost 459.50 MYR per 1,000 plus delivery.
  6. Judgement: skilled staff is key to the service; depends on enough local demand and whether staff/materials can be brought in.

Evaluate · June 2024 · Paper 2 · Regional (R) · Q4(c)

  1. Factors: skilled labour and infrastructure (road, rail, sea access).
  2. Case: luxury shoes, clothing, perfume, wedding outfits; most suppliers in Italy, a few in Spain and China; new factory needs skilled employees.
  3. Chain 1: locate near skilled workers in Italy -> high quality for a luxury brand with awards -> protects 'JC' image.
  4. Chain 2: good transport links -> lower costs of moving materials in and goods out to shops in Canada, UK, Ireland, Australia.
  5. Counter: skilled labour in Italy may be expensive, raising costs; cheaper locations like China may lack the same skill or harm brand image.
  6. Judgement: skilled labour first for a luxury brand; depends on cost difference and whether quality can be kept elsewhere.
