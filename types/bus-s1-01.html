Business objectives: financial, non-financial and social

Financial, non-financial and social objectives a business sets, why a business sets them, why they change as it evolves, and the effect on a business of holding a social objective.

Type 4 1 Business activity and influences on business BUS-S1-01

★★★★★ · 16 of 32 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

The question asks for an aim or objective (financial, non-financial or social), why a business sets objectives, why they change, or the effect of holding a social objective.

Knowledge

  • An objective is a target a business sets so it knows what it is trying to achieve.
  • Financial objectives are about money: survival, profit, sales (revenue), market share and financial security.
  • Non-financial objectives are not mainly about money: social objectives, personal satisfaction, challenge, independence and control.
  • A social objective is an aim to help society or the environment, for example cutting waste or supporting a local community.
  • Setting objectives gives the owner and staff a clear direction, motivates them, and gives a target to check progress against.
  • Objectives change as a business evolves: because of market conditions, new technology, its own results, new laws, or internal reasons such as growth or a new owner.

Before you begin

  • What revenue, costs and profit are
  • What market share means
  • What owners, customers and employees want from a business

Specification: 1.1.1 · 1.1.2

Answer technique: Evaluate and reach a supported conclusion (12 marks) · Explain: point plus two linked expansions (3 marks) · Answer a multiple-choice question · Outline: two linked points in context (2 marks) · State or give a point from the case (1 mark)

Method

  1. Decide whether the objective is financial or non-financial. Why: Many marks, especially multiple choice, depend on sorting it correctly.
  2. Find what the owner or business in the case actually wants, using a detail from the case. Why: State and Outline answers are only credited when they fit the named business.
  3. Link the objective to one effect: on decisions, costs, customers, staff or growth. Why: Explain marks come from the chain of effects, not the label.
  4. For Explain, follow the Explain card: one advantage or reason plus two linked steps. Why: A definition or a list earns only the first mark.
  5. For Evaluate, follow the Evaluate card: weigh how the objective helps (reputation, loyal customers, lower waste) against what it costs (dearer materials, slower growth), then judge. Why: Judgement marks need both sides and a supported conclusion for this business.

How each command word is answered

Command wordTimes askedWhat to write
multiple-choice8Survival, profit, sales, market share and financial security are financial; personal satisfaction, challenge, independence, control and social aims are non-financial.
State7Give one objective, or one reason for setting objectives, written for the named business, for example its product or market, not a general word.
Outline1Name the objective, then add one sentence showing how this business would pursue it.
Explain1Point plus two linked steps, for example social objective, better reputation, more customers. No marks for just a definition.
Evaluate1Weigh how the social objective helps the business grow against its extra costs or limits, using the case, then reach a supported judgement.

Common mistakes

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Calling market share or survival non-financial (both count as financial in these questions).
  • Writing a general objective with no link to the named business in State and Outline answers.
  • Listing several objectives with no explanation, which earns only the first mark.
  • Defining 'social objective' instead of explaining an advantage or effect.
  • Giving a way to measure success (such as a customer survey) when the question asks for an objective.

Worked explanation

  1. Read the command: Explain one advantage, 3 marks, so one point plus two linked steps.
  2. Pick one clear advantage: a better reputation.
  3. Ask 'so what?' twice: reputation leads customers to trust the brand, which leads to more sales.
  4. Use the Samsung case lightly to make the chain concrete; do not just define a social objective.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

One advantage of a social objective is that it can improve the business's reputation. If a business such as Samsung is seen to help society, for example through its aim to make products that 'contribute to a better society', customers are more likely to trust and like the brand. As a result, more people may choose its phones and televisions instead of those made by rivals such as Apple, so its sales increase.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for identifying the advantage (better reputation). 1 mark for the first link (being seen to help society makes customers trust the brand). 1 mark for the consequence (more customers choose it, so sales rise). Total 3.

Grade 9 long answers

Top-level 9-mark Justify and 12-mark Evaluate answers for this type. Written by olevels.com; not official mark schemes.

Evaluate · November 2023 · Paper 1 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define social objectives: aims to help society/environment, not just profit.
  2. Positive 1: green image and Icelandic Design Award -> attracts eco-aware buyers, including online buyers abroad -> sales growth.
  3. Positive 2: low waste, leftovers made into scarves and hats -> lower material cost per item -> more profit to fund growth.
  4. Negative: natural wool costs more -> high prices (jumper 34 000 ISK) -> price-sensitive buyers go elsewhere; long-lasting clothes mean fewer repeat purchases.
  5. Negative: small family workshops may struggle to scale while keeping green standards.
  6. Judgement: mainly positive but slow, steady growth; depends on enough customers paying more for eco clothing and workshops expanding without more waste.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

Social objectives are aims that help society or the environment rather than only making money. AWG's social objectives are to use natural materials such as wool, to make environmentally friendly clothes that last a long time, and to reduce waste.

The first impact is on demand. AWG has won the Icelandic Design Award for its contribution to the environment, and it already sells online to customers in other countries. This gives AWG a strong green brand image. Shoppers who care about the planet may choose AWG instead of fast-fashion brands, so demand rises. Because the website reaches buyers abroad, AWG can grow sales in other countries without opening many new stores, so growth can happen at a low cost.

The second impact is on costs. AWG keeps waste to a minimum and turns leftover material into scarves and hats. This means more products are made from the same material, so the material cost per item falls and extra revenue is earned from the scarves and hats. Higher profit can then be reinvested to fund growth, such as a wider range of baby clothes.

However, the objectives can also slow growth. Natural wool usually costs more than synthetic fabric, so AWG may need high prices; a men's jumper already sells for 34 000 ISK. Price-sensitive shoppers may buy cheaper clothes elsewhere, so sales grow slowly. Also, AWG wants families to buy fewer clothes. If clothes last a long time, each customer comes back less often, so repeat sales fall and AWG must keep finding new customers to grow. In addition, production is in small workshops staffed by family and friends, so making far more clothes while keeping the same green standards may be hard and could raise costs.

Overall, I think the social objectives have a mainly positive impact on AWG's growth, because they explain its award and its distinctive brand, which helps it stand out to online buyers in other countries. Growth is likely to be steady rather than fast. This depends on whether there are enough customers, in Iceland and abroad, willing to pay a higher price for long-lasting, eco-friendly clothing, and on whether AWG can expand its workshops without losing its low-waste methods.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines social objectives (AO1); uses AWG facts: award, online sales abroad, leftover material into hats, 34 000 ISK jumper (AO2); two developed chains on demand and cost (AO3); balanced counter-arguments on price, fewer purchases and workshop capacity, then a supported judgement with a 'depends on' condition (AO4).
