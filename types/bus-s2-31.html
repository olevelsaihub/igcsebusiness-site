Organisational structures: hierarchical, flat, centralised, decentralised

Organisational charts: hierarchical versus flat structures and centralised versus decentralised decision-making, with their benefits and drawbacks.

Type 15 2 People in business BUS-S2-31

★★☆☆☆ · 6 of 32 eligible examination papers, 2019–2025. Difficulty: Medium.

The question mentions a hierarchical, tall or flat structure, an organisational chart, levels of management, or centralised or decentralised decision-making.

Knowledge

  • An organisational chart shows the structure of a business: who is responsible to whom.
  • A hierarchical (tall) structure has many levels, a long chain of command and narrow spans of control.
  • Hierarchical benefits: close supervision and more chances of promotion; drawbacks: slow communication and decisions, and higher management costs.
  • A flat structure has few levels, a short chain of command and wide spans of control: faster communication and lower costs, but managers may be overloaded.
  • Centralised means important decisions are made by senior managers at head office.
  • Decentralised means decisions are passed down to managers in branches or regions, who can react to local customers.
  • Centralised gives control and consistency; decentralised gives speed, local knowledge and more motivated staff.

  • Delegation

Specification: 2.5.1 · 2.5.2

Answer technique: Define a business term (1 mark) · Evaluate and reach a supported conclusion (12 marks) · Explain: point plus two linked expansions (3 marks) · Answer a multiple-choice question

Method

  1. Identify the structure: tall or flat, centralised or decentralised. Why: Each has its own features and effects.
  2. Recall its key feature: number of layers, or where decisions are made. Why: The feature is the identification point.
  3. Link the feature to communication speed, control, cost or motivation. Why: This is the first development.
  4. End with the effect on the business, such as faster decisions or meeting local customer needs. Why: The business consequence completes the chain.
  5. For a 12-mark Evaluate, follow the Evaluate card: weigh control against flexibility for this business and conclude. Why: Evaluation needs both sides and a supported judgement.

Common mistakes

Inferred, not measured: Inferred from the official schemes' credit and rejection patterns; not measured student-error statistics.

  • Mixing up flat and tall structures' features (for example saying a flat structure has a long chain of command).
  • Confusing decentralised (decisions spread out) with flat (few layers).
  • Listing features without explaining the effect on the business.
  • In Evaluate answers, giving only benefits and no judgement.

Worked explanation

  1. Explain card: one benefit plus two linked expansions.
  2. Benefit: few layers of management between the owner and workers.
  3. Expansion 1: messages pass through fewer people, so communication is quicker and clearer.
  4. Expansion 2: decisions are made faster and the business responds quickly to customers.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A flat structure has only a few layers of management between the owner and the workers. This means messages and instructions pass through fewer people, so communication is quicker and less likely to be misunderstood. As a result, decisions can be made and acted on faster, so the business can respond quickly to customers' needs.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for the benefit (few layers). 1 mark for explaining the quicker, clearer communication. 1 mark for the consequence: faster decisions and quicker response to customers.

Evaluate · November 2025 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define centralised structure; state that Samsung's production, design and marketing decisions are made in Seoul.
  2. Benefit chains: consistent quality across 74 countries protects brand and TV leadership; central buying and global marketing give economies of scale and lower unit costs.
  3. Use Figure 3: gross profit $112,000 minus expenses $94,000 leaves $18,000, so cost control matters.
  4. Drawbacks: 270,000 staff and a long chain of command slow decisions; local managers' knowledge of local customers is unused; low authority can demotivate.
  5. Judgement: benefits for major decisions, depending on how much local markets differ; suggest keeping design central but giving some local marketing authority.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A centralised structure means important decisions are made by senior managers at head office. At Samsung, major decisions on production, design and marketing are made in Seoul, South Korea.

Centralisation can benefit Samsung in several ways. First, it gives control and consistency. Samsung aims to create superior products, so when senior managers and skilled engineers in Seoul make design decisions, every factory in its 74 countries works to the same standards. This protects the quality and brand image that help keep Samsung the leading producer of televisions. Second, central buying and marketing can create economies of scale. One decision on a TV design and one global campaign means Samsung can buy parts in bulk and avoid repeating work in each country. This lowers unit costs, allowing lower prices or higher margins. Cost control matters: Figure 3 shows gross profit of $112,000 but other operating expenses of $94,000, leaving operating profit of only $18,000. Third, if the senior team acts quickly, Samsung can respond to new technology and keep up with Apple and Xiaomi in the smartphone market.

However, centralisation has drawbacks for a business this large. With more than 270,000 employees across 74 countries, information and decisions may have to travel up a long chain of command to Seoul, which can be slow. Local managers understand their own customers better, for example which TV sizes or phone prices people in their country want, but they cannot act on that knowledge, so rivals may win those local customers. Managers who have little authority may also feel less motivated, which could lower productivity or cause skilled staff to leave, raising recruitment costs and reducing profit.

Overall, a centralised structure does benefit Samsung for its major decisions, because consistent design and global marketing protect the quality behind its position as the leading TV producer and help keep unit costs down. However, this depends on how different its local markets are. If customer needs vary widely across its 74 countries, Samsung would benefit from giving local managers some authority, for example over local marketing, while keeping design and production decisions in Seoul, as it does now.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines centralisation (AO1), uses Seoul decision-making, 74 countries, 270,000 staff, leading TV producer, Apple/Xiaomi and Figure 3 profit (AO2), develops chains to consistency, economies of scale and speed (AO3), weighs slow chains of command, lost local knowledge and demotivation, then gives a conditional judgement (AO4).
