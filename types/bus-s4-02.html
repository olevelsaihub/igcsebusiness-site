Why businesses carry out market research

Written

Businesses research to understand customer needs, find gaps, reduce risk and inform decisions about product, price, place and promotion. The purpose is the reason, not the method.

Worked explanation

  1. Command: Explain one purpose, 3 marks, so one point and two linked expansions.
  2. Choose the clearest purpose: finding out what customers need.
  3. Link it to an action: designing or adapting the product.
  4. Link the action to a result: more customers buy, so the business is more successful and less likely to fail.
Model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

One purpose of market research is to identify what customers need and want. When the business knows this, it can design its products, or change existing ones, so that they match what customers are looking for. As a result, customers are more likely to buy the products, which increases sales and reduces the risk of the business wasting money on products that do not sell.

How the marks are earned

1 mark for the purpose (identify customer needs). 1 mark for the action (design products to match those needs). 1 mark for the result (higher sales and lower risk of failure).

Evaluate · June 2025 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

Answer plan

  1. Define product trial: test a new product in a few shops before full launch (primary research).
  2. Chain 1: 32,000+ stores in 80 countries -> a failed launch wastes huge amounts on stock, training, promotion -> trial keeps losses small.
  3. Chain 2: feedback protects quality-coffee brand -> improve recipe before launch -> higher sales.
  4. Planning: partners collect feedback; trial sales help estimate orders for all stores.
  5. Counter: small sample may not represent 80 countries; costs time; rivals may copy; slow-growing products may be dropped too early.
  6. Judgement: worth it; depends on a representative sample and acting on results; reduces but never removes risk.
Grade 9 model answer · written by olevels.com, not an official Pearson mark scheme

A product trial means testing a new product in a small number of shops, or with a small group of customers, before launching it everywhere. It is a form of primary market research.

The main reason is to reduce risk. Starbucks has more than 32,000 stores in 80 countries. If it launched a new coffee or cake in every store and customers did not like it, it would waste a huge amount of money on ingredients, staff training and promotion, and unsold stock would be thrown away. By trialling in a few shops first, Starbucks finds out whether customers will actually buy the product. If sales are poor, it can change the recipe or drop the product, so losses stay small.

A second reason is to protect the brand. Starbucks has a passion for high-quality coffee and wants an enjoyable experience for everyone. A poor new product could damage this image and lose loyal customers. Trials let Starbucks collect customers' opinions and improve the product before the full launch, so the product matches what customers expect and sales are higher.

Trials also help with planning. Because Starbucks builds a strong connection between employees and customers, partners can collect feedback directly at the counter. Sales data from the trial helps Starbucks estimate how much to order for all stores and whether the product suits tastes in different countries.

However, product trials have limits. A trial is only a sample; customers in a few shops may not represent customers in 80 different countries, so a product that sells well in one place might fail elsewhere. Trials take time and cost money, and competitors may see the new product and copy it before the full launch. Also, some products become popular slowly, so weak early results might cause Starbucks to drop a product that would have succeeded.

Overall, product trials are worth carrying out, because the cost of a failed launch across 32,000 stores is far greater than the cost of a small trial. However, how useful they are depends on whether the trial shops are a fair sample of Starbucks' customers, ideally in several countries, and on Starbucks acting quickly on the results. A trial reduces risk, but it can never remove it completely.

Why it reaches the top level: Defines product trials as primary research (AO1); uses 32,000 stores, 80 countries, quality-coffee focus and partner-customer link (AO2); developed chains on wasted cost, brand protection and planning (AO3); limits of samples and timing, then a supported conditional judgement (AO4).

Evaluate · November 2021 · Paper 2 · Q4(c)

  1. Define market research: finding out customers' needs and wants to support decisions.
  2. Case: Huawei must keep quality, compete, make profit, meet customer needs and bring new technology; Pro smartphone launched in US at 5 353 CNY (about $799 at 6.70).
  3. Chain 1: research avoids designing a product few people want -> avoids wasted development money that could hurt other products like tablets.
  4. Chain 2: customer feedback on features, size and price -> product fits needs -> more sales, stays competitive.
  5. Counter: fast-changing technology and tastes make research out of date; a truly new product may succeed without research; research costs time and money.
  6. Judgement: worthwhile for an expensive, competitive product; depends on research being recent and well targeted.
